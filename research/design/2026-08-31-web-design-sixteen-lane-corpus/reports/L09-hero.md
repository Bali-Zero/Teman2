## Executive summary

The dateline/dispatch hero is a real, currently-awarded aesthetic — but of the four commercial competitor/analog homepages I actually fetched (Mercury, Boundless, doola, LegalZoom), **zero** put a dateline or masthead on the converting hero; editorial conventions there live on the blog, not the money page. Problem-first copy has real, verified conversion evidence (49%/46% lift, 99% confidence) but only for problem-*aware* audiences — which is exactly what a first-time visa buyer is, so Bali Zero's current H1 is on the right side of the evidence, not just stylistically bold. On a 390×700px viewport nothing below the dateline+H1+one CTA physically fits above the fold — the four doors are guaranteed below-the-fold content by geometry, not by choice, so the H1's only job is to make people scroll. Segmented doors work when categories are self-diagnosable without comparison (Bali Zero's four doors qualify); they fail when they force guesswork between similar-sounding tiers. Hero video is the single most reliable way to blow the LCP/INP budget on the exact device class (mid-range Android, variable signal) this audience uses — and even a funded, professionally built immigration site (Boundless) ships a hero video that silently fails in some browsers.

## 1. Does the editorial/dispatch hero still work in 2026?

But mercury.com's own **hero** reads "Radically different banking" / "Apply online in 10 minutes" — plain benefit copy, no dateline, no byline, no masthead. - **Boundless** (boundless.com), a funded immigration company — hero headline "Here for the journey," two CTA doors, lifestyle video. No dateline, no editorial framing anywhere in the hero. VERIFIED-LIVE (fetched 2026-08-31), https://www.boundless.com/
- **doola** (doola.com), LLC formation for non-resident founders — "Do the Business Side of Things, Better," investor-logo proof strip, zero editorial framing. VERIFIED-LIVE (fetched 2026-08-31), https://www.doola.com/
- **LegalZoom** (legalzoom.com) — "U.S. Legal Help in Indonesia," three service cards, stats proof strip. Editorial *tone* in copy but no masthead structure. VERIFIED-LIVE (fetched 2026-08-31), https://www.legalzoom.com/
- **"AI in Design Report 2026"** — Awwwards Site of the Day, 27 Aug 2026, described in its own listing as "a research report transformed into an editorial digital experience... where data, analysis, and motion speak in a single visual language," built by four named studios (++hellohello, SEB®, Pablo Picart, Ismael Martínez). This *is* a genuine dateline/report-style editorial build — but it is a campaign microsite for a report, not a transactional service homepage; its job is to be read once, not to convert a scared first-time buyer. VERIFIED-LIVE (fetched 2026-08-31), https://www.awwwards.com/sites/ai-in-design-report-2026
- Awwwards' own "Editorial Layout" inspiration gallery, when fetched directly, surfaced agency portfolio pieces tagged "editorial layout / hero font / minimalism" — not named masthead-and-dateline commercial sites. VERIFIED-LIVE (fetched 2026-08-31), https://www.awwwards.com/inspiration/editorial-layout

**The measurable rule.** The pattern across every commercial homepage I checked: editorial *voice* (bylines, section names, magazine taglines) survives on content/blog surfaces; editorial *chrome* (dateline, masthead, "Vol./No.," issue numbers) does not survive on the converting hero of a single company I could verify. Where dateline/masthead structure does show up and win awards, it's on report/campaign microsites whose only conversion event is "keep scrolling," not "hand over a passport and a payment."

**What to steal for Bali Zero.** The dateline is doing something specific and defensible for this audience: it is a trust signal against scam-fear ("this is a currently operating, dated, real outfit — not a template someone spun up last week"), not decoration. Keep it, but treat it as evidence, not aesthetic — pair it with something that proves currency (the "Filed this month: 47 KITAS, 9 PT PMAs" line already does this correctly; a stale dateline would do the opposite — see the "what to avoid" note below).

**What to avoid.** A dateline that isn't live. When I fetched balizero.com directly on 2026-08-31, the summarized dateline text read "Bali Zero · Dispatch · April 2026 · Kerobokan" — five months stale against today's date. (Flagged with lower confidence below — this came through a fetch-and-summarize tool, not a raw HTML read, so the exact string needs a direct byte-level check — but if the date is genuinely static rather than server-rendered to "now," a dateline is worse than no dateline: it converts a trust signal into evidence of exactly the neglect this audience is afraid of. This is the sharpest version of the CONTRACT's own warning — the brief supplies a device (dateline-as-trust-signal), and if implementation doesn't keep it live, it becomes the opposite device (dateline-as-abandonment-signal).

## 2. Problem-first vs. benefit-first headlines

**The named example.** Copyhackers ran a home-page A/B test replacing a benefit-first control with a PAS (Problem–Agitate–Solve) opening that led with the reader's own experience of the problem ("excessive sweaters are sweating for all sorts of reasons. It's almost never 'cos it's hot out.") instead of the product's benefit. Result: **variation B, +49% paid lift; variation C, +46% paid lift, both at 99% confidence.** VERIFIED-LIVE (fetched 2026-08-31), https://copyhackers.com/2016/06/great-home-page-copy/

**The measurable rule.** This isn't "problem-first always beats benefit-first" — it's stage-of-awareness matching (Eugene Schwartz's five-stage model, operationalized by Copyhackers): PAS/problem-first wins specifically with a **problem-aware** audience — people who know they have the issue but haven't yet found (or trust) a solution. Benefit-first and anchoring effects (the classic "100 albums for $5 each" framing result — the larger number presented first sets the anchor for everything after it) win with **solution-aware or product-aware** audiences who are already comparing named options. FROM-MEMORY (unverified) for the anchoring citation's exact study; the awareness-stage mechanism itself is VERIFIED-LIVE via the Copyhackers case study and its explicit framing ("If you have reason to believe that the people you can convert... are in X stage of awareness... you should test messaging for X stage of awareness, not for all the stages").

**What to steal for Bali Zero.** A first-time tourist Googling "Bali visa" at midnight is problem-aware almost by definition — they know moving/staying in Bali involves a visa minefield, they don't yet know Bali Zero exists or who to trust. That is precisely the population PAS testing wins on. The current H1 ("Most people moving to Bali pick the wrong visa in the first month...") is textbook PAS-open, and the evidence above is a real reason to keep it rather than soften it to a benefit line — do not let a stakeholder's discomfort with a "negative" headline talk this back into "Your visa, handled." Use PAS on the **home page** (cold traffic, mixed/low awareness) but flip to benefit/certainty framing on the **Visa Oracle verdict page**, where the visitor has already self-diagnosed and needs reassurance, not agitation — the CONTRACT's own description of that surface ("the verdict, the exact price, a named human") is already benefit/certainty-first, and that's correct for its funnel position.

**What to avoid.** Applying PAS forever, past the point the visitor already believes the problem. On GARUDA VOA — a self-serve flow for people who already know exactly what they want (a VOA, fast) — a problem-agitation opener would be actively counter-productive; that surface should open on certainty (fixed price, government fee included) not warning. The fad-version of PAS is generic fear ("Don't get scammed!") untethered to a specific, checkable fact — Bali Zero's version names the actual statute (PP 18/2021) and a specific failure mode (wrong visa, lease that doesn't hold up, tax-time surprise), which is what separates evidence-based PAS from clickbait fear.

## 3. The first 390×700px on a phone

**The named example / measurable rule.** Nielsen Norman Group's original eye-tracking study (57,453 fixations, 21 users, 541 pages) found **80.3% of viewing time above the fold, 19.7% below** — but that number was measured at a **1024×768 desktop** resolution, a viewport more than double the area of a 390×700 phone screen and from an era before mobile-majority traffic. VERIFIED-LIVE (fetched 2026-08-31), https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention-original-research/. A secondary aggregator citing NN/g (undated, no direct citation link) gives a different, more recent-sounding split of **57% above / 43% below** — I could not verify which NN/g study, or which year, that figure actually comes from; treat it as directional, not load-bearing. VERIFIED-LIVE (fetched 2026-08-31) for the aggregator page itself, but its NN/g attribution is unverifiable, https://www.omniconvert.com/blog/below-the-fold/. What NN/g's own site *does* say directly, in plain language, and which I can stand behind: **"on a phone, the fold sits high and most of the page is below it."** That line is the actually load-bearing, current, NN/g-sourced fact for mobile.

**The arithmetic that matters more than either percentage.** At a 390×700–844px viewport (iPhone-class, browser chrome subtracted), you get roughly one dateline line, a two-to-three-line H1 at a legible size (≥28-32px per common mobile-type guidance), and — if the H1 is long, as Bali Zero's warning-style H1 deliberately is — barely one CTA button before the fold line. **Four segment doors do not fit above the fold on this viewport at any readable type size.** This isn't a design choice to relitigate; it's geometry. That reframes the whole "fold" question for this surface: the H1 + dateline's only job on mobile is to earn the scroll to the doors, not to expose the doors.

**What to steal for Bali Zero.** Stop treating "get the doors above the fold" as a goal on mobile — it is unachievable at this H1 length and not worth truncating the H1 to chase. Instead, budget the visible 390×700px for exactly three things and nothing else: dateline (trust signal), H1 (the warning that earns the scroll), one CTA or a visible scroll affordance. Measure success with real scroll-depth analytics on the live domain (Contentsquare/GA4 scroll events), not with a fold percentage borrowed from a 2010 desktop study — that specific instrumentation gap is listed below in "what I could not verify."

**What to avoid.** Redesigning the hero to cram a compressed 2-word version of the H1 plus a shrunk four-door grid into the visible viewport "to be safe." That produces the classic fad failure mode: four illegible tap targets in a viewport that was never going to fit them, at the cost of the one sentence actually earning the scroll. The fold is not dead as a concept (NN/g's above/below viewing-time gap is real and large in every version of the number I found), but "fold" ≠ "everything important" — it means "the thing that earns the next fold."

## 4. Segment doors — self-selection navigation

**The named examples, with real numbers from the doors themselves.**

- **Boundless** — **two** doors in the hero itself: "For Business" / "For Individuals." Narrow, binary, sits inside the primary viewport. VERIFIED-LIVE (fetched 2026-08-31), https://www.boundless.com/
- **LegalZoom** — **three** doors as service cards below the headline and proof strip: Business formation / Wills and trusts / Intellectual property. VERIFIED-LIVE (fetched 2026-08-31), https://www.legalzoom.com/
- **doola** — **zero** doors in the hero. Pure benefit statement + single CTA funnel ("Get Started for Free"); segmentation (e-commerce vs. service business, which state, etc.) happens inside the product after signup, not on the homepage. VERIFIED-LIVE (fetched 2026-08-31), https://www.doola.com/
- **Bali Zero** itself — **four** doors (moving / business / taxes / property).

**The measurable rule.** Hick's Law formalizes reaction/decision time as **T = b·log₂(n+1)** for equally-probable choices (Hick 1952, 10-lamp Morse-key experiment; generalized by Hyman 1953 to entropy-weighted choices, T = bH). VERIFIED-LIVE (fetched 2026-08-31), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hick%27s_law. The critical, easy-to-miss detail: it's **logarithmic**, so going from 2 doors to 4 doors adds far less decision time than going from 4 to 8 — but the formula assumes options that are *effortful to distinguish*. Iyengar & Lepper's well-known 2000 jam-tasting study is the classic demonstration of when more choice actively hurts: a 24-jam display converted roughly a tenth as well as a 6-jam display (commonly cited as ~3% vs. ~30% purchase rate). FROM-MEMORY (unverified exact percentages — I could not re-fetch the original paper this session; a WebSearch summary independently surfaced "4%" and "31%," close but not identical to the figures I recall, which itself is a reason to flag rather than state either version as certain).

**The variable Hick's Law alone doesn't capture, and that Bali Zero should lean on:** jam flavors require *tasting effort* to tell apart — the choice itself is the cognitive load. "I'm moving to Bali" vs. "I already live here and need taxes" vs. "I'm buying property" require **zero comparison effort** — a visitor already knows which bucket they're in before they land on the page; the door is a label-match, not a decision. That is why Boundless (2 doors), LegalZoom (3), and Bali Zero (4) all sit comfortably under the failure threshold the jam study demonstrates, while a B2B SaaS pricing page with "Growth / Scale / Enterprise" tiers routinely does not — those categories require the visitor to evaluate unfamiliar, overlapping criteria (seats? volume? support tier?) before they can even select, which is exactly the jam-study failure mode.

**What to steal for Bali Zero.** Keep four doors — it sits inside the evidence-backed range demonstrated by two real fetched analogs (2 and 3 doors) and the mechanism (self-diagnosable, MECE, zero-comparison-effort categories) that explains why those didn't paralyze. Do not add a fifth (e.g., splitting "business" into "PT PMA" vs. "local company") on the homepage — that reintroduces exactly the comparison effort ("which one am I?") that breaks the mechanism; push that distinction one click deeper, inside the "starting a business" door.

**What to avoid.** doola's zero-door pattern looks tempting (simpler hero, cleaner metrics) but it works there because doola has a **single** service line (US LLC formation) wearing different feature bundles — there's nothing to segment on the homepage because there's only one "door" to begin with. Bali Zero has four genuinely different regulatory products (immigration, company law, tax, property law); collapsing to one CTA the way doola does would force every visitor through one generic message, the exact anti-pattern the B2B navigation research above warns against ("buying committees do not share context... bounce increases").

## 5. Hero motion and imagery — the Core Web Vitals cost

**The numbers, verified against web.dev directly.** LCP: **good ≤2.5s, needs improvement 2.5–4.0s, poor >4.0s**, measured at the **75th percentile** of real page loads, split by device class. VERIFIED-LIVE (fetched 2026-08-31), https://web.dev/articles/lcp. INP: **good ≤200ms, needs improvement 200–500ms, poor >500ms**, same p75 methodology. VERIFIED-LIVE (fetched 2026-08-31), https://web.dev/articles/inp. Real-world 2026 pass rates (May 2026 CrUX data, 18.4M origins): **only 55.9% of all origins pass all three Core Web Vitals; mobile passes at 48% vs. desktop at 56%**, an 8-point mobile penalty gap. VERIFIED-LIVE (fetched 2026-08-31), https://www.digitalapplied.com/blog/core-web-vitals-benchmarks-2026-pass-rate-reference

**The mechanism specific to hero video.** Per web.dev, a `<video>` element itself is **not** an LCP candidate — but its poster image or first painted frame **is**. So the real cost of an autoplay hero video isn't "the video counts against LCP" directly; it's that the video file starts downloading immediately on page load and **competes for bandwidth and main-thread time with whatever actually is the LCP candidate** (hero image, web font, large heading text) — on a constrained device this delays the thing Google is actually grading. This nuance is easy to get backwards and is worth stating precisely rather than repeating the shorthand "video hurts LCP."

**A concrete, verified failure case in this exact vertical.** Boundless — a funded immigration company, direct analog to Bali Zero — ships a hero video that returned, on fetch, as "a professional video element (noted as unsupported in some browsers)." VERIFIED-LIVE (fetched 2026-08-31), https://www.boundless.com/. This is a real, observed instance of hero video degrading ungracefully at a company with real engineering resources, in the same product category.

**The connectivity context, corrected.** I expected to find "slow Bali connection" data; the verified number runs the other way. Indonesia's median mobile download speed via cellular was **45.01 Mbps** as of late 2025, up 53.1% year-on-year. VERIFIED-LIVE (fetched 2026-08-31), https://datareportal.com/reports/digital-2026-indonesia. That is a *national median*, not a guarantee for a specific villa's indoor signal or a tourist roaming on a throttled international eSIM — but it means the CONTRACT's framing of "slow Bali connection" is more safely read as a **device/CPU** problem (mid-range Android parsing JS, decoding images, painting layout — the actual driver of the 48%-vs-56% mobile INP gap above) than a raw-bandwidth problem. That changes the fix: it isn't primarily "compress the video harder," it's "reduce main-thread work and defer anything non-critical until after the LCP element paints."

**What to steal for Bali Zero.** A still hero image (or pure type, no image at all) gives a deterministic, cacheable LCP candidate — no bandwidth contention, no main-thread video-decode competition, no "unsupported in some browsers" failure mode. Given the H1 is doing the persuasive work (see §2), and the CONTRACT explicitly wants "investigative-journalistic" not "hospitality," pure type with no hero image at all is defensible and *cheap to keep fast* — the newspaper-front-page reference the CONTRACT itself invokes typically leads with type and a single still photograph, not motion.

**What to avoid.** Any hero treatment (video, WebGL, scroll-jacked animation) that is not measured against a mid-range-Android/throttled-CPU trace before shipping — "we tested it on my M-series laptop" is not a Core Web Vitals test. If motion is used at all, it should be CSS-only (no video file, no WebGL canvas) and deferred until after the LCP element has painted, never blocking it.

## 6. The fad already exhausted

**The named tell.** Fetched directly from a 2026 web-design-trends piece: **kinetic/scroll-driven typography** — viewport-scaled (`vw`) headline type whose weight and width are mapped to scroll position — is presented alongside the claim that "static headers are becoming obsolete." VERIFIED-LIVE (fetched 2026-08-31), https://fireart.studio/blog/the-best-web-design-trends/

**The measurable tell that it's already exhausted, not merely popular.** Two things in that same source, together, are the actual signal: (1) the totalizing framing itself — "X is becoming obsolete" is exactly the kind of claim that shows up simultaneously across every trend-forecast article in a season, which is the industry-level version of the CONTRACT's own observed failure mode ("whatever the brief supplies, the models return" — here the brief is the entire design-blog corpus, and every studio is reading the same brief); and (2) the **same article** names a second, closely-related 2026 trend — **bento-grid layouts** — and immediately flags it as an "operational paradox": it breaks on mobile collapse and amateur implementations double DOM weight through duplication. A trend that arrives and gets its failure mode documented in the same publishing cycle is a trend that peaked before it shipped widely. VERIFIED-LIVE (fetched 2026-08-31), same source.

**What to steal for Bali Zero.** Nothing directly — this finding is a warning, not a component. The tell to watch for going forward: if a hero pattern is being pitched as a differentiator and it also appears, unprompted, in three or more of this month's design-trend roundups, its differentiation value is already gone by the time it ships.

**What to avoid.** Scroll-jacked, viewport-scaled hero type on Bali Zero's H1 specifically — the H1 is a warning sentence meant to be *read and understood on first paint* by a frightened, possibly non-native-English-reading visitor on a small screen; kinetic type that reflows on scroll actively fights legibility and directly conflicts with the mobile-fold finding in §3 (the H1 has to do its persuasive work before any scroll interaction happens, not through one). The second-order fad worth flagging even though I could not verify a fresh 2026 source for it directly (FROM-MEMORY, unverified) is generic AI-generated hero photography/illustration — glossy, textureless, interchangeable-looking imagery that by 2025–2026 was widely enough recognized ("AI slop") that its presence reads as a negative trust signal for exactly the audience Bali Zero has (people already primed to distrust polish that could be fabricated). A still photograph of an actual Kerobokan office or an actual filed document is the opposite instinct and fits both the "no hospitality fluff" voice rule and the scam-fear audience concern in the CONTRACT.
