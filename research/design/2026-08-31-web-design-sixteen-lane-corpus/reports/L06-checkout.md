## Executive summary

QRIS on a web checkout is a *display-and-wait* pattern (show QR → user leaves your page into their own app → webhook completes it), and Bank Indonesia's own MDR rule already forbids Bali Zero from passing that fee to the buyer — build the price as genuinely all-inclusive, because the regulator enforces it. QRIS Cross-Border only recognizes bank/e-wallet apps from Malaysia, Thailand, Singapore, Japan, South Korea and China — a US/European/Australian tourist (the actual GARUDA VOA audience) cannot QRIS-pay with their home banking app at all; they need a Gojek/OVO e-wallet funded from an international card first, and the checkout copy must say so or it will read as broken. Virtual Account is an *async, leave-the-page* payment — the single highest-leverage design move is showing bank-specific paste-ready instructions plus a live "we are watching for your payment" state, not a "please wait" spinner. Baymard's 2026 numbers (70.22% cart abandonment, 40% citing surprise costs, 18% forced-account-creation) map directly onto GARUDA's four-question flow, and the fix is almost entirely about field count and guest-checkout clarity, not visual polish. The multi-day wait for Immigration is the real design problem nobody in payment UX solves well — Uber's three wait-psychology principles (and their measured 11% cancellation drop) transfer better to a visa tracker than any fintech pattern does, because GOV.UK itself has no dedicated "track my application" component to copy.

## Finding 1 — QRIS is a redirect-out-of-page payment, and Bank Indonesia bans passing its fee to the buyer

**The named example.** Bank Indonesia's own explainer of the QRIS Merchant Discount Rate, published on bi.go.id, and Bank Indonesia's QRIS Tap NFC rollout (via en.wikipedia.org/wiki/QRIS, cross-checked against Bank Indonesia's press materials).

**The measurable rule.** The UX mechanism is exactly what the brief guessed: the merchant page renders a static QR image (usually a `<canvas>`/`<img>` generated server-side from the QRIS payload string), the buyer leaves the browser into their own banking or e-wallet app to scan or (as of March 2025) NFC-tap, confirms with their own app's PIN/biometric, and the merchant's backend only finds out via an asynchronous webhook/notification — there is no synchronous "success" the browser can show without polling or a webhook-driven push. QRIS Tap (NFC) launched March 2025 for Android first, iOS later, letting a buyer tap phone-to-reader instead of camera-scanning — relevant mainly for in-person, not web checkout. On fees: Bank Indonesia's page states plainly, quoted directly: *"Biaya MDR ini ditanggung oleh merchant dan tidak boleh dibebankan kepada konsumen"* (the MDR is borne by the merchant and must not be charged to the consumer). Effective 15 March 2025: micro merchants (UMI) pay 0% up to Rp 500,000 and 0.3% above it; small/medium/large merchants pay 0.7%; education 0.6%; fuel stations 0.4%; and — notably for Bali Zero's business shape — public-service and government-fee-adjacent categories (including passport-related services, per BI's own category list) sit at 0%. **VERIFIED-LIVE (fetched 2026-08-31)**: https://www.bi.go.id/id/publikasi/ruang-media/cerita-bi/Pages/mdr-qris.aspx · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/QRIS

**What to steal for Bali Zero.** On the GARUDA VOA payment step: never show a raw "Processing…" spinner after QRIS is chosen — show the QR immediately with an explicit instruction line ("Open GoPay, OVO, DANA, ShopeePay or your bank app → Scan → Confirm") because the buyer is about to alt-tab away from you, and the copy needs to prepare them for that, not let them think the page froze. Because MDR can never be surfaced to the buyer, "IDR 790.000, all-inclusive" is not just marketing language — it's the legally accurate description of what BI enforces, so the page can say it with a straight face.

**What to avoid.** Don't build a fake real-time "Waiting for scan…" progress indicator that has no actual signal behind it (most integrations only get a webhook on final payment, not on "QR scanned") — an indicator that never moves for 90 seconds erodes trust faster than an honest static state with a manual "I've paid" fallback button.

## Finding 2 — QRIS Cross-Border does not include Bali Zero's actual buyer

**The named example.** Bank Indonesia's QRIS Cross-Border rollout timeline (Wikipedia's QRIS entry, cross-referencing Bank Indonesia and ASEAN Briefing reporting) and Wise's own foreigner payment guide.

**The measurable rule.** QRIS Cross-Border is a bilateral interoperability agreement, country by country, not a universal foreign-card rail: Thailand (full rollout August 2022), Malaysia (May 2023), Singapore (November 2023), Japan (August 2025, the first non-ASEAN partner), with South Korea and China announced/in progress for 2026. It works by letting a *resident of one of those specific countries* scan an Indonesian QRIS code using *their own domestic* banking/e-wallet app (DuitNow in Malaysia, PromptPay in Thailand, NETS/PayNow in Singapore) — the interoperability is between national QR schemes, not a generic "any tourist" gateway. A tourist from the US, UK, Australia, EU, or anywhere outside that named list has no domestic app that will scan an Indonesian QRIS code. Their only path, confirmed directly by Wise's guide, is registering a local e-wallet (GoPay, OVO, DANA) using an international phone number, linking an international Visa/Mastercard to top it up, and paying QRIS *from that wallet* — which typically also wants an Indonesian SIM for the OTP verification step, or a workaround via international roaming/eSIM data. **VERIFIED-LIVE (fetched 2026-08-31)**: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/QRIS · https://wise.com/au/blog/indonesia-qr-code-payment

**What to steal for Bali Zero.** This is the single most important payment-UX fact for GARUDA VOA: since the buyer may be "a foreigner abroad with a foreign card" who has never set foot in Indonesia yet, QRIS should be presented as the option for "already in Bali with GoPay/OVO/DANA," never as the default or first-listed option for someone paying from abroad — for that buyer it requires app-install-plus-KYC friction *before* they can even see the QR do anything, which will read as a broken checkout if QRIS is the first thing they see. Virtual Account and card should lead for the "abroad" segment; QRIS should lead for the "already here" segment — segment the checkout by the passport-upload step's implied context, not by defaulting to whichever payment method is technically first in the gateway's SDK.

**What to avoid.** Don't advertise "Pay by QRIS" as a universal, borderless feature on the homepage or VOA landing copy — it is accurate only for six named nationalities' banking apps plus anyone who has already set up a local e-wallet, and overclaiming it is exactly the kind of "guaranteed"-adjacent overreach the brief already forbids for legal claims.

## Finding 3 — Virtual Account is an async, leave-and-come-back payment; the screen's job is to survive the gap

**The named example.** Midtrans's BCA Virtual Account API documentation, Xendit's hosted-checkout product page, and DOKU's checkout-page product page.

**The measurable rule.** VA has a fixed lifecycle the screen must represent honestly: (1) a virtual account number is generated and shown, (2) the default expiry is 24 hours unless the merchant overrides it (Midtrans's own docs: minimum 20 seconds, maximum 180 days — the merchant chooses), (3) the buyer leaves your page to pay via ATM, mobile banking, or internet banking, and (4) confirmation reaches the merchant via webhook — not by the buyer clicking anything on your page. Every major Indonesian gateway (Midtrans, Xendit, DOKU) renders per-bank step-by-step instructions (ATM menu path, mobile-banking menu path, internet-banking menu path) because the payment flow genuinely differs by bank and channel, and getting even one menu label wrong sends the buyer down a dead end with no in-app recovery. Xendit's own product page states it adds "reminder emails to increase payment rates and reduce cart abandons" specifically because VA is abandon-prone — the buyer closes the tab intending to "pay later from the bank app" and then doesn't. **VERIFIED-LIVE (fetched 2026-08-31)**: https://docs.midtrans.com/reference/bca-virtual-account · https://www.xendit.co/en/optimize-conversion-with-xendit-hosted-checkout/ · https://www.doku.com/en-us/products/payment-gateway/checkout-page

**What to steal for Bali Zero.** On the GARUDA VOA payment screen, once VA is chosen: (a) a large, tap-to-copy VA number — never a number the buyer has to retype by hand into a banking app; (b) a visible countdown to expiry, because "24 hours" said once in prose gets forgotten the moment the buyer alt-tabs to their bank app; (c) collapsible per-channel instructions (ATM / m-banking / internet banking) rather than one undifferentiated paragraph; (d) since polling for a webhook can lag, an explicit page state that says "we're watching for your payment, this page will update automatically" plus an email/WhatsApp confirmation as the actual reliable delivery channel — don't make "did it work?" depend on the buyer keeping a tab open.

**What to avoid.** Don't render a bare VA number with no expiry and no per-bank instructions and call it done — that's the version that looks "technically correct and emotionally flat," which is precisely the failure the brief already logged once for this project.

## Finding 4 — 2026 checkout fundamentals: field count, guest checkout, and mobile keyboards still decide conversion

**The named example.** Baymard Institute's Cart & Checkout Usability research (14 years, 344 benchmarked sites, 25 rounds of usability testing with 4,400+ sessions) and its dedicated mobile-checkout study.

**The measurable rule.** Baymard's headline 2026 abandonment figure, from its own published list, is **70.22%** average cart-abandonment rate across aggregated studies; among shoppers who weren't just browsing, the top cited reasons are extra costs revealed too late (40%), delivery-speed concerns (20%), card-security worry (19%), **forced account creation (18%)**, and process complexity (17%). Baymard separately estimates the average large e-commerce checkout can gain a **35.26%** conversion lift by fixing documented, solvable usability issues, and rates 65% of sites it audits as "mediocre or worse." On mobile specifically, Baymard's own testing found that **60% of the top 50 mobile-optimized e-commerce sites fail at least 2 of 5 basic keyboard-type optimizations** (wrong `type`/`inputmode`, missing `autocorrect="off"`/`autocapitalize="off"`), that a touch keyboard consumes 50% of visible screen in portrait and 70–80% in landscape, and that in qualitative testing 60% of subjects struggled to even locate the guest-checkout option when it wasn't presented as a clearly separate, equally-weighted choice. Separately, web.dev's form-best-practices guide (Google's own team) specifies the exact `autocomplete` tokens that make browser/OS autofill actually fire: `cc-number`, `cc-name`, `cc-exp-month`, `cc-exp-year`, `name`/`given-name`/`family-name`, `address-line1`/`address-line2`, `tel`, `email` — and to use `type="text" inputmode="numeric"` for card numbers rather than `type="number"` (which adds unwanted spinner arrows). **VERIFIED-LIVE (fetched 2026-08-31)**: https://baymard.com/lists/cart-abandonment-rate · https://baymard.com/research/checkout-usability · https://baymard.com/blog/mobile-checkout · https://web.dev/articles/payment-and-address-form-best-practices

**What to steal for Bali Zero.** GARUDA VOA's "4 questions → price → passport upload → payment" is already close to Baymard's ideal shape (short, linear, no forced login), which is a genuine asset — protect it. Concretely: label guest/no-account flow explicitly rather than implying it (Baymard's 60% figure is about *findability*, not willingness); use `inputmode="numeric"` and the exact `autocomplete` tokens above on every field (passport number, name, card fields); never make total price change between question 4 and the payment screen — Baymard's #1 reason (40%) is exactly a late-revealed total, and GARUDA's whole pitch is "all-inclusive," so a static, identical number from quote to receipt is not a nice-to-have, it's the product claim being kept.

**What to avoid.** Multi-column checkout forms on a 360–390px viewport (the brief's stated audience device) force horizontal scrolling or illegibly cramped fields — single column, one field group visible at a time, is not a stylistic choice here, it is what the screen width physically allows.

## Finding 5 — the multi-day wait needs a tracker built on honesty about uncertainty, not a fake progress bar

**The named example.** GOV.UK Design System's own pattern library (which has *no* dedicated "track my application" pattern despite governing the UK's entire digital-government estate), Wise's transfer-tracking product, and Uber's published wait-psychology research.

**The measurable rule.** This is the most transferable, least "fintech" finding in the lane. Uber's UX team documented three mechanisms that reduce perceived wait and cancellation, tested in production: **Idleness Aversion** (people tolerate waiting better when shown *something* happening than a static screen); **Operational Transparency** (showing *why* an estimate is what it is, not just the number, increases trust in it); and the **Goal Gradient Effect** (visibly marking progress accelerates perceived completion). Combined in an A/B test on Uber Pool, these produced a measured **11% reduction in post-request cancellation rate**. Wise's transfer tracker deliberately uses plainer-language labels in its consumer UI than in its own API status enum (`outgoing_payment_sent` becomes "Completed" on-screen) and claims it delivers over 80% of transfers within 2 hours of its own displayed estimate — an estimate it can stand behind, not a padded worst case. USCIS's own case-status tool is the negative example: it only updates on discrete milestones, so weeks of real adjudication happen with no visible signal, and applicants routinely misread silence as inaction — the exact failure mode any "waiting for Immigration" tracker must design around. **VERIFIED-LIVE (fetched 2026-08-31)**: https://uxmag.com/articles/how-uber-uses-psychology-to-perfect-their-customer-experience · https://design-system.service.gov.uk/patterns/ (checked for, and confirmed absent, a dedicated status-tracking pattern) · https://wise.com/help/articles/2941900/when-will-my-money-arrive **FROM-MEMORY (unverified)**: Wise's 80%-within-2-hours figure and its label-remapping practice, via search summary, not independently re-fetched verbatim.

**What to steal for Bali Zero.** Build the GARUDA VOA/Visa Oracle post-payment tracker on three moves: (1) name the stage the buyer is actually in ("document review" / "submitted to Immigration" / "decision pending") using plain language, never a raw backend status string; (2) say explicitly what is *not* known yet ("Immigration doesn't publish a fixed timeline; most VOA e-visas clear in N–M days" — stated as a range with its own source, not a false countdown); (3) push the update to email/WhatsApp the moment state changes, so the tracker page is a convenience the buyer can check, not the only place they'll ever learn the outcome — this directly answers the brief's own instruction to use messaging "rather than making the user re-check a page."

**What to avoid.** A fake animated "in progress" bar that has no real milestone behind its motion (the Idleness Aversion research supports *some* motion mitigating anxiety, but motion that implies false precision — e.g., a bar that appears to be "80% done" with no actual basis — is the dishonest version, and it's the version that breaks trust the moment the buyer notices it never actually moves for three real days).

## Finding 6 — a foreign card in IDR carries DCC temptation, default-on 3DS friction, and issuer-side declines you cannot fix at the gateway

**The named example.** Xendit's own 3D Secure (3DS2) documentation and its card-decline/error-code reference; a Bank Mandiri ATM fee change (foreign-card Rp 50,000 access fee from 22 February 2026) surfaced during this research as a related but distinct cost signal.

**The measurable rule.** Xendit's docs state authentication is **enabled by default** for Indonesian card acceptance — merchants must actively opt into an unauthenticated flow, and even then the *issuing* bank, not the Indonesian acquirer, makes the final risk call on whether to demand a challenge. The challenge step is an OTP sent by the buyer's own bank via SMS or their banking app, rendered on a page **hosted by the issuing bank, not by the merchant or gateway** — Bali Zero's checkout has zero control over that screen, and any support issue there can only be resolved by contacting the buyer's own bank. On declines, Xendit's error-code reference confirms the generic **"Do Not Honor" (code 05)** is the catch-all an issuing bank returns for anything from insufficient funds to a typo to fraud suspicion — the merchant page cannot distinguish these and should not guess in its error copy. Xendit separately claims (unaudited, on its own page) a client saw a **198% improvement in card acceptance** after switching to its hosted checkout — treat as a directional gateway-choice signal, not a guaranteed number. On currency: no gateway page fetched here recommended or warned against Dynamic Currency Conversion; the DCC risk sits entirely on the buyer's *own* card issuer or ATM offering to bill in their home currency at a worse rate, not something Bali Zero's checkout triggers — but its copy can warn against it. **VERIFIED-LIVE (fetched 2026-08-31)**: https://docs.xendit.co/credit-cards/authentication/3ds · https://docs.xendit.co/docs/cards-card-declines-and-error-codes · https://www.xendit.co/en/optimize-conversion-with-xendit-hosted-checkout/ **FROM-MEMORY (unverified)**: the general DCC "3–7% FX margin" figure and ATM/merchant-side DCC mechanics, from search-summarized fintech content, not a card-network primary source.

**What to steal for Bali Zero.** Set the copy expectation *before* the card step, not after a decline: "your bank may show this charge in your own currency — if offered a choice, choose to pay in IDR, not your home currency, for the best rate" (a direct, honest warning against DCC, which meaningfully differs from most fintech pages that stay silent on it). For the OTP/3DS step, tell the buyer in advance that a text/app prompt from *their own bank* is coming, since a challenge screen appearing mid-checkout with no warning is the single most common reason an unprepared buyer abandons at that exact step. For decline copy, never claim a specific cause ("insufficient funds") the gateway itself cannot confirm — say "your bank declined this card; contact them or try a different payment method," and prominently offer QRIS/VA as the fallback path rather than a second card retry, since a second attempt on the same card after a "Do Not Honor" rarely succeeds.

**What to avoid.** Silently enabling DCC-adjacent card-brand routing that lets the *card network* offer the buyer a "pay in your home currency" toggle at the payment step — some acquirer configurations expose this as an opt-in the merchant can suppress; Bali Zero should suppress it, because it directly contradicts the "the price is the whole price" promise the brief opens with.

## Finding 7 — the fad that measurably hurts conversion right now: countdown timers and urgency devices the buyer has learned to distrust

**The named example.** The pattern is documented across e-commerce urgency-marketing analyses of 2025–2026 (not a single named product, but a repeatedly measured behavior).

**The measurable rule.** A countdown timer that resets on page reload, or urgency copy with no real deadline behind it, is now specifically named in 2025–2026 UX writing as the most over-used and most-tested-for scarcity device: research summarized across multiple 2026 conversion-marketing sources describes shoppers actively refreshing a page to check whether a "limited time" timer resets (a behavior described as common enough to be a named test pattern), and states that shoppers who catch a fake deadline lose trust in that merchant *permanently*, not just for that session. The same research reports genuine, deadline-backed timers can lift conversion 20–35% — the mechanism (urgency) is not inherently bad, only the fabricated version of it. Regulators are moving on this too: the US FTC's 2025/2026 enforcement update to its Negative Option Rule explicitly targets deceptive urgency and subscription-trap dark patterns, with the UK CMA opening parallel investigations into algorithmically-generated manipulative claims. **FROM-MEMORY (unverified)**: the specific conversion-lift and "60% test by refreshing" figures are drawn from search-summarized 2026 marketing-blog content (scandiweb.com, funnelish.com, easyappsecom.com and similar), which this research pass did not independently WebFetch-and-quote from a primary study — treat the *direction* (fake urgency destroys trust; genuine urgency can help) as solid, and the specific percentages as indicative rather than citable.

**What to steal for Bali Zero.** Nothing invented — if a real constraint exists (e.g., "Immigration's e-VOA queue processes faster before a specific cutoff time WITA"), state it plainly with the actual mechanism, once, without a ticking widget. Given the brief's explicit brand stance — "never guaranteed approval," an audience already primed to distrust visa agents as scam-adjacent — any fabricated urgency device on GARUDA VOA's payment screen is not just a wasted conversion trick, it actively confirms the exact fear (scam operator) the whole redesign exists to dispel.

**What to avoid.** Any countdown timer, "X people are viewing this," or "only N slots left today" device on the VOA payment page that isn't wired to a real, checkable fact — this audience is explicitly named in the brief as scam-wary, making this the single fastest way to convert a hesitant buyer into a lost one.
