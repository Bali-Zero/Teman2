# CHAPTER: The Real Estate Developer

**1. The Dream**

Daniel didn't fall in love with Bali. Daniel fell in love with the *spread*.

He was honest about it, at least with himself. He'd made his money in property—mid-market residential developments outside Lisbon, then a couple of bolder plays in the Algarve—and he had a developer's eye, which is a specific and slightly ruthless way of looking at land. Where a tourist saw a rice paddy at golden hour, Daniel saw a parcel: access road here, six villas there, a shared pool, a price-per-unit that would make his Portuguese investors weep with joy. He had watched Bali land values climb for a decade with the helpless fascination of a man watching a train he keeps just missing.

This time he wasn't going to miss it. The plan was clean and it was big: acquire land, *develop* it—build out a cluster of residences designed for sale, not just for his own use—and do the whole thing through a proper foreign-investment vehicle so it was bankable, investable, and exitable. Not a guy with one villa. A *developer*. A real estate development company that bought land, built homes, and sold them.

He'd found the code that matched the ambition exactly. **KBLI 68111, Aktivitas Pengembangan Bangunan dan Lahan Hunian**—development of residential buildings and land. The official description was almost a mirror of his business plan: purchase and sale of residential buildings, development of houses and apartments for more permanent use, development of residential building projects *for sale*. It was, on paper, the perfect code for a man who builds homes to sell them. A hundred percent open to foreign ownership nationally.

He had investors lined up. He had a parcel in mind. He was moving fast—the way developers always move, because in property, the spread belongs to whoever closes first.

He did not know that he was racing a door that was, at that very moment, beginning to swing shut.

**2. The Reality Check**

The consultant didn't reach for the "Bali Smile." This time the expression was different—tighter, more urgent. The look of someone watching a man walk quickly toward an elevator whose doors are closing.

"68111," he said. "Residential development for sale. You've picked the code that exactly describes what you do. Nationally it's a hundred percent open—you're right about that. And right now, *today*, in Bali, it is not hard-blocked. The system will let a PMA register it." He paused. "But I need to be very precise with you, because precision is the whole story here. This code is *proposed for closure* to foreign PMAs in Bali. It is on the list. The door is not shut. The door is *closing*."

Daniel leaned forward. "Proposed. So it's open."

"It is open," the consultant agreed. "But 'open and proposed for closure' is a different animal from 'open and stable.' Let me show you the company it keeps." He pulled up two other codes. "Management consulting—70209—was *also* just an open code, until the twenty-eighth of January 2026, when it became the first door to slam shut in Bali. Then the broad moratorium landed on the thirteenth of May—Governor letter B.27.000/642/PM/DPMPTSP—and swept whole risk classes off the board overnight. The pattern on this island, for the last year, has been one direction only: doors closing. Real estate development for foreigners is squarely in the crosshairs—it's one of the most politically sensitive activities here, because nothing inflames the local conversation like foreigners buying land and building homes to sell. 68111 is *proposed* for closure. That word is not a comfort. It's a countdown."

"How long?"

"Nobody can tell you that honestly," the consultant said. "That's the nature of a proposed closure—there's no published countdown clock. It could remain open for a while. It could close on a provincial decision with very little warning, the way 70209 did. What I can tell you is the asymmetry of your bet. If you build your entire investment thesis—your investors' money, a multi-year development timeline, units you intend to sell two and three years from now—on a code that is *currently* open but *proposed* for closure, you are taking a regulatory risk on top of every normal development risk. Construction takes years. A proposed closure can land in months. You could be holding half-built inventory under a code that's been closed to new foreign activity, in a province that has shown, repeatedly, that it will close these doors."

Daniel, who closed deals for a living, recognized the feeling of a deal that was more fragile than it looked. "So it's not 'no.'"

"It's not 'no,'" the consultant said. "It's 'not yet—and moving the wrong way for you.' Which, for a developer betting investor capital on a multi-year build, can be more dangerous than a clean 'no.' A clean no, you walk away from. A 'closing' you can talk yourself into."

**3. The Blueprint: The Property-Development Fact Sheet**

There is a category of Bali risk that is more insidious than the blocked code, and it is the *closing* code—the activity that is technically registrable today but proposed for closure tomorrow. It is more dangerous precisely because it isn't a wall. A wall stops you cleanly. A closing door tempts you to sprint through before it shuts, with years of construction and investor capital riding on a piece of timing no one can promise you.

Here is the verified, ground-truth reality—stated with the precision this case demands.

> ### THE KBLI BATTLEGROUND: RESIDENTIAL DEVELOPMENT
>
> *Crucial context: the code below is 100% open to foreign investment on the National Investment List. The provincial situation is time-sensitive and must be stated exactly—the Bali Moratorium (Governor letter B.27.000/642/PM/DPMPTSP, effective 13 May 2026) blocks "low" and "medium-low" risk classes, and separately, certain politically sensitive activities are being proposed for closure to foreign PMAs.*
>
> **THE CLOSING DOOR**
> *   **68111 — Aktivitas Pengembangan Bangunan dan Lahan Hunian (Development of Residential Buildings & Land):**
>     *   *National Status:* 100% PMA Open.
>     *   *Bali Status:* **PROPOSED FOR CLOSURE — not yet a hard block.** As of now, this code is *not* hard-blocked; a PMA can still register it. But it is *proposed* for closure to foreign PMAs in Bali. This is a status in motion, not a stable open door.
>     *   *Critical distinction:* "Proposed for closure" ≠ "closed." It also ≠ "safely open." It means the door is currently passable but on the list to be shut, with no published timeline.
>     *   *Scope note:* The description covers purchase/sale of residential buildings, development of houses/flats/apartments for permanent use, and development of residential building projects for sale.
>
> **WHY THIS STATUS IS UNIQUELY DANGEROUS**
> *   Property development is one of the most politically sensitive foreign activities in Bali. The direction of provincial policy over the past year has been one-way: closure.
> *   Precedent: 70209 (management consulting) went from open to *closed* on a single date (28 Jan 2026). A "proposed" closure can become an actual one with limited warning.
> *   The mismatch that bites developers: construction runs for *years*; a proposed closure can land in *months*. Building a multi-year, investor-funded thesis on a closing code stacks regulatory risk on top of development risk.

**4. The Money**

Real estate development is the most capital-intensive dream in this book, and the most exposed to the timing risk above. Beyond the PMA minimum paid-up capital, a developer is deploying serious money up front: land acquisition (as a foreign vehicle, via leasehold or Right-to-Build structures, not freehold ownership), paid as large lump sums; then a full development build—multiple structures, infrastructure, access, shared amenities; then the building permits (PBG) for a commercial residential project, environmental assessments, and the marketing-and-sales machinery to actually move the units. The cost ranges from large to enormous depending on the parcel and the number of units.

The "closing door" status poisons the financial model in a way that's worth spelling out, because it's where developers fool themselves. The capital is committed *over years*. Land is bought now; units sell in two or three years. If the code closes to new foreign activity midway, you are not just facing a paused application—you may be holding partially-developed inventory and forward-sale commitments under a regulatory framework that has shifted beneath you. The "Bali Tax" of informal, communal costs is heavy for any large build, but the real exposure here is the *regulatory* tax: the cost of having built your spread on a foundation that the province may withdraw. No financial model should treat "currently open" as "open for the life of the project."

**5. The Timeline**

This is the longest timeline in the book and the one most lethally mismatched with its regulatory status. A residential development—land, permits (PBG), build-out of multiple units, sales—is a multi-year endeavour even before the rainy season, the Galungan and Kuningan labour exodus, and the Java-port material delays do their work. From land acquisition to selling the final unit, you are realistically looking at a span of *years*.

And that is precisely the danger. The development clock runs in years; the closure clock may run in months. There is no published date for when "proposed" becomes "actual," which means you cannot plan around it—you can only weigh it. A developer who registers 68111 today is betting that the door stays open long enough to acquire, build, and sell. Given the one-way direction of provincial policy and the precedent of codes closing on a single decision, that is a bet against the trend. The honest timeline conversation isn't "how long will my build take?"—it's "will the door still be open when my build is half-finished?"

**6. The Bali Angle**

No business in this book sits more directly on the island's rawest nerve than this one. The Bali Angle for the property developer is not a quaint note about *banjar* relations and *Melaspas* ceremonies—though those apply with full force, and a development that ignores the customary village council or skips the blessing of the land courts disaster. The deeper angle is that *foreigners developing land to sell* is the single most charged issue in Bali's relationship with foreign investment. The land is sacred, finite, and ancestral. Every rice paddy converted to villas is, to many Balinese, a small permanent loss. The moratorium and the targeted closures are, at their heart, the island drawing a line about who gets to profit from its soil.

A developer who understands this doesn't fight it—he reads it as the most important market signal available. The "proposed closure" of 68111 is not a bureaucratic accident; it is the island telling foreign developers, as clearly as a regulation can, that this door is the one it most wants shut. Building your thesis in the teeth of that message is not bold. It's deaf.

**7. The Pitfall**

The pitfall here is the seduction of the closing door—the developer's conviction that he can outrun it. Daniel's whole professional instinct, the instinct that made him money, was to move fast and capture the spread before someone else does. That instinct is exactly what makes the "proposed for closure" status so dangerous to him. A blocked code would have stopped him cleanly. A closing code whispers: *it's still open, go now, beat the deadline.* And there is no deadline he can actually see—only a direction.

The specific trap is treating "currently registrable" as "safe to build a multi-year, investor-funded development on." It is not the registration that's at risk—he could probably register today. It's everything *after*: the years of construction and forward sales exposed to a closure that could land at any point, with no warning, in a province that has demonstrated it will close these doors on a single decision.

The crucial lesson: **distinguish, ruthlessly, between a door that is open-and-stable and a door that is open-but-closing.** They look identical at the moment of registration and could not be more different over the life of a project. For a status marked *proposed for closure*—especially in the most politically sensitive activity on the island—the right question is never "can I get in before it shuts?" It is "what happens to my committed capital if it shuts while I'm inside?" Verify the live, current status before every major commitment, because a proposed closure today is a precedent that became a hard block on a single date for the consultant before him.

Daniel's spread is real. The math, in a vacuum, works. But he is racing a door that is closing in the one direction his whole project cannot survive—and the only winning move may be to read the message the island is sending and not run for the elevator at all.

**8. The Way Through**

Daniel's whole instinct was to find the door that *exactly* matched his business plan and sprint through it. That instinct is what put him in front of a closing door. The way through is the opposite reflex: stop chasing the one code that names your ambition, and ask a colder question — *what is the legal, stable activity adjacent to my goal that the island is not trying to shut?* A developer's value lives in three things: the ability to design, the ability to build, and the ability to manage and move units. None of those, on their own, is "a foreigner acquiring land and selling homes" — and that distinction is exactly where the open doors are. You move *up* the chain (design, engineering), *sideways* (build for others), or you *partner* (a local owns the asset; your PMA earns from the expertise around it).

> ### THE OPEN ROUTES — VERIFIED ALTERNATIVES
>
> *Every code below is currently registrable for a foreign PMA in Bali — none is hard-blocked and none is on the "proposed for closure" list, unlike 68111. None of them is a back door to the blocked activity. But "registrable" is not "unconditionally open": several of these — especially the real-estate **service** codes (68210, 68292, 68299) — sit in a higher-risk band that can carry foreign-ownership caps or local-participation conditions, and each still carries its own sector permits. Treat each as a genuinely different business, and verify its live DNI/PMA status and any ownership condition for your exact activity before you commit. They let you stay in the property world without betting investor capital on the one door the island most wants shut.*
>
> **ROUTE 1 — Sell the design, not the development (71101 — Aktivitas Arsitektural · 71109 — Aktivitas Enjinering dan Konsultansi Teknis Terkait Lainnya).** Move *up* the value chain. Instead of a PMA that owns land and sells finished homes, run a PMA that does the architecture and engineering for residential and commercial projects — yours by contract, or other people's. This is the part of "development" that is pure expertise, and it is currently registrable. Honest caveat: professional services like these require the relevant technical/professional licensing and registered qualified personnel; "registrable for a PMA" is not "open without a sector permit."
>
> **ROUTE 2 — Build for owners, don't develop-to-sell (41019 — Konstruksi Konvensional Gedung Lainnya · 41020 — Konstruksi Prapabrikasi Bangunan Gedung).** A construction PMA builds structures under contract for whoever owns the land and the project. This is the "build out a cluster" muscle of your plan, sold as a service rather than as your own speculative inventory. Honest caveat: construction is a regulated sector — it requires the construction business licence (SBU) and the right classification/qualification before you can lawfully take work. Note also that purpose-built *residential* construction sits closer to the sensitive line than other building types; scope your contracts deliberately and verify each project's status, because a construction code does **not** itself grant you the right to acquire land and sell residential units.
>
> **ROUTE 3 — Own the program, let a local own the storefront (68292 — Pengelolaan Real Estat Hunian Atas Dasar Balas Jasa/Fee atau Kontrak · 68299 — Aktivitas Real Estat Atas Dasar Balas Jasa/Fee atau Kontrak Lainnya).** This is the honest partner structure, not a nominee dressed up. A local entity (a UMKM, a landowner) holds the asset and the land-facing activity that is closing to foreigners. Your PMA earns on a transparent fee-or-contract basis for managing the residential real estate — the part that is genuinely a service and is currently registrable. Honest caveats, two of them: first, these are real-estate *service* codes that sit in a higher-risk band — verify their live DNI/PMA status and any foreign-ownership-cap or local-participation condition before you build on them, because "registrable" here is not "guaranteed condition-free." Second, the dividing line on substance is real: your PMA must actually perform and be paid for management, and it must **not** be the disguised beneficial owner of the land or the sales. This only works as a true arm's-length service agreement; if the substance is "foreigner controls the land and the profit," you are back in nominee territory (see below).
>
> **ROUTE 4 — Sell the deal-flow, not the dirt (68210 — Aktivitas Jasa Intermediasi Real Estat).** Real-estate intermediation — brokering, matching buyers and sellers, advisory on transactions — is currently registrable and is a legitimate way to monetise your developer's eye and your investor network without owning or developing the asset yourself. Honest caveats: this is a real-estate *service* code in the higher-risk band, so verify its live DNI/PMA status and any foreign-ownership-cap or local-participation condition for your activity first; and it is brokerage, not development — it does not let you build and sell your own inventory, and it carries its own licensing for real-estate intermediation activity.
>
> **WHAT IS NOT A ROUTE**
> *   **Nominee.** Putting the land or the development company in a local name while you hold the real control and the real money is illegal, unenforceable, and the single most-prosecuted structure on this island. Route 3 is the *opposite* of this — it works only when the local genuinely owns and you genuinely provide a paid service.
> *   **Code-mismatch.** Registering an open construction or consulting code and then quietly running "acquire-land-and-sell-homes" under it is the exact trap chapters 2–7 warn about. The activity, not the code on the certificate, is what gets audited. An open code does not launder a closing activity.
> *   **Fake consulting.** A "design" or "management" PMA that does no design and no management, and exists only to receive the development profit, is a nominee with extra steps. If the substance isn't real, the structure isn't legal.
>
> A closing door is the island speaking. The disciplined move is not to find a cleverer way through the same door — every clever way is some flavour of nominee — but to walk through a *different* door that is currently open *and* more stable, run the business it actually names, and verify each code's live status and conditions first. Sell the design. Build for owners. Manage what a local owns. Broker the deals. Each of these keeps you in property, keeps your capital legal, and keeps you out of the one fight the province has decided to win.
