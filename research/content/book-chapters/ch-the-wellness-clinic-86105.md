# CHAPTER: The Wellness Clinic

**1. The Dream**

Dr. Elena Vasquez had spent fifteen years watching wellness happen to other people. As an attending physician in a Madrid public hospital, she had treated the burned-out, the over-medicated, the chronically inflamed—and she had developed a quiet heresy: that the line between *medicine* and *wellbeing* was an artificial border drawn by insurance companies, and that somewhere on the right island, she could erase it.

Bali was the obvious place. Half the planet was already flying there to be healed, and most of what they found was either rigorous medicine with no warmth, or warmth with no rigor—crystals and good intentions and nobody checking your blood panel. Elena's dream lived precisely in the gap: a real clinic, doctor-led, with proper diagnostics, IV therapy administered by actual nurses, hormone and longevity protocols grounded in lab work, physician consultations that took an hour instead of seven minutes. *Evidence-based wellness.* Medicine with a soul, in a sarong.

And—because she was a complete vision, not a half one—she pictured the second half of the building. A serene wing where, after their consultation, clients would drift into a beauty and aesthetics lounge: facials, advanced skincare, lash and brow work, the cosmetic finishing touches. Heal them clinically, then make them feel beautiful. One seamless journey from the lab to the lounge. One business. One roof.

She had her codes mapped, of course. The clinic: **KBLI 86105, Aktivitas Klinik Swasta**—private clinic. The beauty lounge: **KBLI 96220, Perawatan Kecantikan**—beauty care. Two codes, two wings, one beautiful integrated sanctuary of clinical-grade wellbeing.

She did not yet know that the regulation would let her be a doctor but forbid her from running a beauty salon—and that the line between them was not where she thought it was.

**2. The Reality Check**

The consultant in Renon listened to Elena's whole vision before he said a word. When she finished, he did something unusual—he complimented her.

"This is, genuinely, one of the cleaner plans I've heard," he said. "Because the heavy half of your dream—the *clinic*—is on the right side of every line. Let me show you."

He pulled up **86105.**

"Aktivitas Klinik Swasta. Private clinic. Primary and follow-up healthcare—outpatient, inpatient, the clinical work. When a foreign-owned company registers this, the system treats it as a Large Enterprise, and at that scale a private clinic is rated medium-high to high risk. That makes total sense—you're handling patients, diagnostics, regulated procedures, real medical liability. And high risk is exactly what you want here, because the Bali moratorium—the one that took effect on the thirteenth of May 2026, Governor letter B.27.000/642/PM/DPMPTSP—only blocks the *low* and *medium-low* risk classes. Your clinic lives above that line. **It is registrable.** The doctor-led, evidence-based wellness clinic you described? You can build it."

Elena allowed herself a breath of relief. "And the beauty wing?"

The consultant's expression changed. He pulled up **96220.**

"This is where the line bites, and it bites in a way that surprises everyone. *Perawatan Kecantikan*—beauty care. And the official description is very specific: it covers beauty work 'tidak dilakukan oleh dokter spesialis'—work *not* performed by a medical specialist. Nail studios, lash extensions, lash lifts, manicures, cosmetic beautician services. The non-medical, aesthetic-finishing half of your dream."

"Right. The lounge."

"The lounge is blocked," he said. "Not by the moratorium, this time—by something more fundamental. In the OSS system, 96220 has *no Large-Enterprise scale row at all.* The activity is reserved for small and micro local enterprises—Perpres 49/2021, the list of fields kept for UMKM. A PT PMA is, by definition and by law, a Large Enterprise. So a foreign-owned company simply *cannot* register 96220. There's no door to walk through. The category was deliberately set aside for Balinese small businesses, and a foreign PMA doesn't fit through it anywhere in Indonesia, let alone in Bali."

Elena sat very still. "So I can run a medical clinic—the harder, riskier, more regulated thing—but I cannot run a nail-and-lash lounge."

"Exactly that," the consultant said. "And the logic, once you see it, is almost elegant. The *clinical* work requires a doctor, real liability, real infrastructure—it's high-risk, capital-heavy, and the province is happy to let a serious foreign clinic raise the standard of care. The *cosmetic* work requires none of that—low barrier, high volume, perfect for a local family business. So the regulation reserves it for locals. The fine line in your building isn't medical-versus-spa. It's: *does a doctor have to do it?* If yes, you can probably own it. If no, it's likely been set aside for Balinese enterprise."

**3. The Blueprint: The Clinical-vs-Cosmetic Fact Sheet**

The wellness founder's fatal assumption is that "health and beauty" is one industry. The Indonesian classification system disagrees violently. There is a hard border running through the middle of every wellness building, and it is not the border you'd draw. It is the line between *what a doctor must perform* (high-risk, foreign-ownable) and *what a beautician performs* (reserved, often, for local micro-enterprise).

Here is the verified, ground-truth reality.

>
> *Crucial context: both codes below are 100% open to foreign investment on the National Investment List. The divergence is structural and provincial—the Bali Moratorium (Governor letter B.27.000/642/PM/DPMPTSP, effective 13 May 2026) blocks every "low" and "medium-low" risk class for PMAs island-wide; and separately, some activities have no Large-Enterprise scale at all, reserving them for local UMKM.*
>
> **THE CLINIC — ALIVE**
> *   **86105 — Aktivitas Klinik Swasta (Private Clinic):**
>     *   *National Status:* 100% PMA Open.
>     *   *Bali Status:* **REGISTRABLE.** At the Large-Enterprise scale every PMA carries, a private clinic is rated medium-high/high risk—above the moratorium line. The doctor-led, diagnostics-and-treatment clinic survives. (Expect heavy medical licensing: facility permits, practitioner credentials, health-ministry compliance.)
>     *   *Scope note:* The description covers primary and follow-up care—outpatient and inpatient—including occupational-health clinics.
>
> **THE BEAUTY LOUNGE — CLOSED TO PMA**
> *   **96220 — Aktivitas Perawatan Kecantikan dan Perawatan Kecantikan Lainnya (Beauty Care):**
>     *   *National Status:* 100% PMA Open.
>     *   *Bali Status:* **BLOCKED — closed to any PMA.** The OSS system has no Large-Enterprise scale row for this code; the activity is reserved for small/micro enterprises (Perpres 49/2021 Annex). A PT PMA, being a Large Enterprise by law, cannot register it—in Bali or anywhere in Indonesia.
>     *   *Scope note:* Explicitly the *non-medical* beauty work—nail studios, lash extensions, manicure/pedicure, beautician services "not performed by a medical specialist."
>
> **THE DIVIDING LINE**
> *   The border is not "medical vs spa." It is **"must a doctor perform it?"** If the activity is genuinely clinical (doctor-led, regulated), it tends to be high-risk and foreign-ownable. If it's cosmetic and non-medical, it tends to be reserved for local micro-enterprise—off-limits to a PMA.

**4. The Money**

A clinic is a serious capital proposition—the heaviest of the two halves Elena wanted to build. Beyond the standard PMA minimum paid-up capital, a private medical clinic carries real infrastructure cost: medical-grade fit-out, diagnostic equipment, licensed practitioners on payroll, facility permits, and health-ministry compliance that scales with the high-risk classification. The leasehold on a suitable building—paid as a lump sum for the full 25- or 30-year term, not monthly—adds to a build that ranges from substantial to serious depending on the diagnostics and inpatient capacity.

The cruel financial twist is what it does to Elena's integrated dream. She had mentally cross-subsidized the two wings: the high-margin beauty lounge would soften the heavy capital of the clinic. But the lounge is off the table entirely as a foreign-owned business. The clinic must stand on its own economics. And the workaround people reach for—"I'll just have a local partner own the beauty wing"—is a genuine structure (a separate locally-owned company under common arrangement), but it is a real legal undertaking with real governance, control, and tax implications, not a casual side-room. It is the kind of thing you design with a lawyer from day one, not bolt on after you've signed the lease.

**5. The Timeline**

A medical clinic is one of the longer hauls on the island, because medical licensing sits on top of the normal corporate and building timeline. You need the PMA, the building permit (PBG) for a clinical facility, environmental and health-facility approvals, and practitioner credentialing—each a multi-step process. A realistic timeline from decision to first patient is best measured in many months, with the medical-licensing layer being the long pole.

The beauty wing, by contrast, has no timeline at all *as a PMA*—because there is no application to file. The honest planning question for Elena was never "how long until the lounge opens?" but "how do I restructure so the lounge is locally owned and legally clean, or do I drop it and let the clinic stand alone?" That decision, made early, saves months; made late—after the building is leased around a two-wing layout—costs them.

**6. The Bali Angle**

A wellness clinic in Bali is selling something the island has an enormous, complicated stake in: healing. Bali's identity as a place of restoration is centuries old and deeply sacred—and also, lately, heavily commercialized by foreigners. A foreign-owned clinic that arrives with real medical rigor can genuinely raise the standard of care; a foreign-owned operation that drapes ordinary cosmetic services in spiritual language and prices locals out is exactly what the province is increasingly guarding against. The reservation of 96220 for local micro-enterprise is part of that guardianship—keeping the accessible, lower-barrier beauty work in Balinese hands.

The *banjar* will know your clinic intimately—medical waste, patient traffic, ambulance access, the comings and goings of a healthcare facility all touch the community directly. And before you treat your first patient, the building will receive its *Melaspas* blessing. A clinic, of all businesses, benefits from being understood as a place of care that respects the spiritual fabric of where it stands—not a foreign machine extracting wellness from a sacred island.

**7. The Pitfall**

The pitfall that nearly cost Elena her integrated layout was the assumption that "health and beauty go together"—that because the clinic and the lounge served the same client on the same journey, they shared a regulatory fate. They do not. The classification system runs a hard border between them, and it is precisely the *easier* half—the cosmetic, non-medical, low-barrier work—that is closed to her, while the *harder* half is open.

This inverts every instinct. Foreigners expect the risky, regulated, capital-heavy thing to be the hard one to get permission for, and the gentle spa services to be the easy add-on. In Bali it is the reverse: the high-risk clinical work survives because it's high-risk, and the low-risk beauty work is blocked because low-risk, low-barrier categories are exactly what the province reserves for locals.

The crucial lesson: **in wellness, check each activity against the dividing line of "must a doctor perform it?"—and never assume that the easier service is the easier registration.** A foreign-owned clinic can do extraordinary clinical work in Bali. It cannot run the nail bar next door. Design the building, the company, and the partnerships around that border from the very first sketch—not after the lease is signed around a fantasy of one seamless roof.

Elena can be a doctor in paradise. She cannot, as a foreigner, be a beautician. And understanding *why* that line falls where it does is the difference between a clinic that opens and a dream that stalls in the OSS system.
