# CHAPTER: The Software Developer

**1. The Dream**

Sam had read this book backwards.

Not literally—but he had come to Bali having already absorbed every horror story. He knew about the villa guys who got blocked. He'd heard about the consultant whose code closed first. He had a friend-of-a-friend who'd nearly registered against some phantom creator code and lost a deposit. By the time Sam sat down to plan his own move, he had developed a kind of pre-emptive dread, a conviction that the island was a regulatory trapdoor designed specifically to swallow optimistic foreigners.

Which made what he wanted to build feel almost too good to be true.

Sam built games. Specifically, he led a small, brilliant, scattered team—two engineers in Berlin, an artist in Manila, a sound designer in Lisbon—who made tight, beautiful indie games and the tooling around them: a game engine plugin, a netcode middleware library that other studios licensed, the occasional work-for-hire build for a bigger publisher. He'd been running it as a tangle of personal contracts and a dormant UK company, and he was tired of it. He wanted one clean home base, one real entity, somewhere the team could fly in for sprints, somewhere he could actually *live*. Somewhere with surf at the bottom of the hill.

A *PT PMA*. A real Bali software studio. **KBLI 62110, Pengembangan Video Gim, Perangkat Lunak Video Gim**—development of video games and game software. He'd found the code, double-checked it against the current 2025 classification (he'd learned *that* lesson secondhand), confirmed it was a real, live code, and confirmed it was a hundred percent open to foreign ownership nationally.

But he'd been burned by optimism before. So he walked into the consultant's office braced for the catch. *There's always a catch,* he thought. *Tell me where the trapdoor is.*

**2. The Reality Check**

The consultant looked at the code, looked at Sam's tense, defensive posture, and almost laughed—not unkindly.

"You're waiting for me to ruin your day," she said.

"I've read the stories. The villa, the café, the consultant. I know how this goes. So just tell me. Where does 62110 die in Bali?"

"It doesn't," she said.

Sam blinked.

"62110—game and game-software development—is one of the doors that *stays open*," she went on. "Let me show you why, because the reason matters. When your PMA registers, the system treats it as a Large Enterprise. And at that scale, software development is rated medium-high to high risk in the OSS system. The Bali moratorium that took effect on the thirteenth of May 2026—Governor letter B.27.000/642/PM/DPMPTSP—only blocks the *low* and *medium-low* risk classes. Your code sits comfortably above that line. **It is registrable.** The clean, real, Bali-based software studio you want to build? You can actually build it."

For a moment Sam didn't trust it. "That's it? No trapdoor?"

"There's a *catch*, but it's not a trapdoor," she said. "It's the same fact from the other side. The reason 62110 survives the moratorium is that it's classified as *higher-risk*—and higher-risk classification is not free. It comes with a heavier licensing and compliance obligation than you might expect for what feels like, you know, people typing code in a nice office. The thing that keeps the door open is the same thing that makes the room more demanding once you're inside. You'll have real licensing steps, real compliance reporting, real obligations that a 'low-risk' business wouldn't carry. The good news and the homework are the same sentence."

Sam exhaled, slowly. "So the catch is... I actually have to do it properly."

"The catch is that 'open' doesn't mean 'easy,'" she said. "It means *allowed*. You're allowed. Now you have to earn the license. But yes—after everything you've read, here's a chapter where the answer is: go ahead. Build it. Just build it correctly."

**3. The Blueprint: The Software Fact Sheet**

After five cautionary tales, it's worth stating the relief plainly: not every door in Bali is closed. The moratorium is a scalpel, not a wall—it targets *low and medium-low risk* PMA activities specifically. Genuinely higher-risk categories, including real software and technology development, pass through. But "open" is not a synonym for "effortless." The very risk classification that keeps the door open is what makes the licensing inside it heavier.

Here is the verified, ground-truth reality.

**4. The Money**

A software PMA is, mercifully, one of the lighter capital builds in this book—no land lease for a hotel, no commercial kitchen, no medical-grade fit-out. You'll commit the standard PMA minimum paid-up capital, the notary and formation fees, and the OSS registration. The physical footprint is whatever you want it to be: a desk in a co-working space at the low end, a proper studio with a meeting room and a wall of monitors at the high end. The build-out cost ranges from "almost nothing" to "a nice office," entirely at your discretion.

Where the money does show up is in the catch. The higher-risk licensing and compliance obligations carry cost—not enormous relative to a clinic or a beach club, but real, and ongoing, and easy to underestimate when you've budgeted as if you were just renting desks. The honest number for a software studio is: cheap to *form*, modest to *run*, but with a compliance line item that a low-risk business wouldn't have. Plan for it rather than being surprised by it.

And a word on the perennial temptation: the virtual-office shortcut. You cannot register a PMA against a virtual office as its Bali domicile—virtual offices are banned for that purpose here. Even the lightest software company needs a genuine, verifiable address. The "I'll just use a mailbox" plan does not survive contact with the OSS system.

**5. The Timeline**

This is one of the faster timelines in the book—and a welcome one after the villa's eighteen months and the clinic's medical-licensing marathon. With no heavy building permit (PBG) for a large structure and no environmental impact assessment, the path is mostly corporate formation plus the higher-risk licensing layer. In the good case, that's measured in weeks to a couple of months rather than the better part of a year.

The licensing layer is the variable. Because 62110 is higher-risk, its license is not a rubber stamp—it's the part of the timeline most likely to stretch if your scope is unclear or your documentation is thin. The teams that move fast are the ones who walk in with their exact activity scope nailed down, so the licensing step has nothing to query. The teams that drift are the ones who registered hoping to "figure out the scope later." Decide what you actually build *before* you file, and the open door swings quickly.

**6. The Bali Angle**

The software founder's Bali Angle is subtle, because your business doesn't touch the land or the temples the way a villa or a clinic does. You're not pouring concrete or treating patients. But you are doing something the island increasingly values and watches: bringing *high-value, high-skill, capital-light work* to Bali—and the province's whole strategy with the moratorium is to steer foreign investment *toward* exactly this kind of activity and away from the low-barrier businesses it wants to reserve for locals. You are, in a sense, the kind of foreigner the policy is designed to welcome.

That welcome comes with a quiet expectation: that you actually contribute. A real studio that hires and trains Indonesian engineers, that brings genuine technology transfer, that pays its taxes and its *banjar* contributions, is participating in the island's economy rather than just sitting on top of it. The digital studio that flies in, extracts cheap rent and good wifi, and trains no one is the version the island grows tired of. Even with no concrete and no patients, your building will still receive its *Melaspas*—and the community will still notice whether you are a guest who gives or a guest who only takes.

**7. The Pitfall**

The pitfall in this chapter is not a closed door—it's the *complacency* that an open door invites. Sam walked in braced for catastrophe and walked out with a green light, and the danger in that moment is real: the relief of "it's open!" can make a founder skip the homework. "Open" is the most misread word in Indonesian business law. It means *allowed*, not *easy*, not *automatic*, not *done*.

The specific trap for the software founder is treating the higher-risk licensing as a formality. The very classification that saved you from the moratorium is the one that imposes a heavier compliance load—and founders who registered thinking "it's just software, how regulated can it be?" discover the licensing and reporting obligations after the fact, when they're harder and more expensive to satisfy retroactively. The catch isn't hidden; it's just easy to ignore when you're celebrating.

The crucial lesson: **when a Bali door is open, walk through it properly—not casually.** Verify your exact scope maps to 62110, budget for the higher-risk licensing and ongoing compliance, secure a real (not virtual) domicile, and decide precisely what you build before you file. The moratorium is a scalpel; it deliberately leaves room for serious technology businesses. Be one. Build the studio. Just build it correctly—because in Bali, even the open doors expect you to earn the room behind them.

After five chapters of walls, Sam's is the one where the answer is yes. Don't waste it by treating yes as the end of the work. It's the beginning.
