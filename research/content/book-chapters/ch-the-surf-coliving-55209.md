# CHAPTER: The Surf-Coliving

**1. The Dream**

Finn had been chasing two things his entire adult life—waves and a way to never leave them—and in Canggu he finally saw how to fuse them into a business. A surf-mad software developer from Cornwall who'd gone remote in 2020 and never gone back, he had spent four years bouncing between coliving spaces from Costa Rica to Sri Lanka, and he had developed strong opinions about all of them. Most were either soulless dorms or overpriced resorts pretending to have community. None nailed the specific magic he wanted: a place where remote workers and surfers lived together, worked in the morning, surfed at dawn and dusk, and built the kind of friendships you only get when you share a kitchen and a lineup.

His vision was precise and beautiful. A converted compound in Pererenan, ten minutes from a forgiving beach break. Twelve rooms, a shared workspace with real desks and real wifi, a communal kitchen, a plunge pool, boards racked by the door, and a rotating community of nomad-surfers paying a premium monthly rate for the lifestyle and the people. Part hostel, part coworking, part surf camp, all community. He'd seen the numbers on integrated lifestyle accommodation in Bali—the premiums were real, the demand insatiable.

He came to the consultant's office in board shorts, salt still in his hair, radiating the easy confidence of a man who'd already mentally moved in. "Surf-coliving," he said. "Short-stay rooms, coworking, community. I think the accommodation part is 55209—other short-term accommodation. Let's set up the PMA so I can lock the lease."

**2. The Reality Check**

"Don't lock the lease yet," the consultant said. "55209—*Aktivitas Penyediaan Akomodasi Jangka Pendek Lainnya*, other short-term accommodation—is the code for exactly what you're describing: bungalows, hostels, capsule hotels, B&Bs, the coliving end of lodging. And it's a problem for you."

"Blocked?"

"Effectively closed for the model you just described—but for a subtler reason than the café or the gym. The reservation that bites here is written by *name*, not by code. Annex II of Perpres 10/2021, as amended by Perpres 49/2021, allocates *Guest House* lodging to cooperatives and small local enterprise—and Pasal 5(5) of the same Perpres scopes that reservation to exactly the activity named in the annex, not to the whole KBLI code around it. 55209 is the code where that named activity lives. So the question OSS is really asking you is: *what is your scope?* If the scope you declare reads as the guest-house model—rooms, shared facilities, short stays—you are standing on the reserved line, and a PT PMA cannot take a reserved *bidang usaha*; and whether your scope reads that way is OSS's call, not yours. And here's the part that catches every foreigner: a PT PMA is *by law* a large (Besar) enterprise, regardless of how small your actual operation is—you cannot shrink your way under a reservation built to protect the small."

Finn frowned. "But it's tiny. Twelve rooms. I'm the opposite of a big corporation."

"Your *operation* is tiny. Your *legal classification* is not. This is the single most counterintuitive trap in Indonesian company law, and it kills more small accommodation dreams than the moratorium does. The reserved lines—homestay (55201), villa (55203), and the guest-house scope of this very coliving code (55209)—were set aside for Indonesian small business. A foreigner can only operate through a PMA. A PMA is automatically Besar. Besar is exactly what a reservation for the small excludes. The size of your dream is irrelevant; the structure of your vehicle is everything."

"So the accommodation half is dead."

"The accommodation half, owned by your PMA and declared as what it actually is, is effectively dead—OSS would have to read your scope as something other than the reserved guest-house line, and betting a locked lease on that reading is not a plan. But like the retreat founders, your business is really two businesses stapled together—lodging and coworking—and they have different, separately ugly verdicts. Let's look at both before you despair, because the shape of the workaround depends on the seam between them."

**3. The Blueprint: The Coliving Fact Sheet**

The trap that destroys small-accommodation founders is invisible because it's not about risk or moratorium—it's about *scale law*. The dream is small; the legal vehicle is large; and the codes are reserved for the small. The mismatch is fatal and has nothing to do with how modest your actual plans are.

Here is the verified, ground-truth reality.

> ### THE KBLI BATTLEGROUND: COLIVING & SHORT-STAY
>
> *Crucial context: all codes below are 100% open on the National Investment List. The obstacles are the Perpres Annex II reservations (lines of business allocated to cooperatives/MSMEs) plus Bali's moratorium.*
>
> **THE STRUCTURALLY CLOSED DOOR — THE LODGING**
> *   **55209 — Aktivitas Penyediaan Akomodasi Jangka Pendek Lainnya (Other Short-Term Accommodation):**
>     *   *National Status:* 100% PMA Open.
>     *   *Bali Status:* **SCOPE-DEPENDENT — the reserved line runs through the middle of the code.** Annex II of Perpres 10/2021 as amended by 49/2021 (p.15, entry 48) allocates *Guest House* lodging to cooperatives/MSMEs, and Pasal 5(5) scopes the reservation to the named activity only. A PT PMA cannot take the reserved guest-house scope; whether the rest of the code is open depends on the scope actually declared—verify live on OSS before committing.
>
> **THE COWORKING HALF**
> *   **68127 — Office Building Management (where coworking lives):** **PROPOSED FOR CLOSURE** to foreign PMA in Bali (real-estate cluster). Not a clean home for the work-space half either.
>
> **THE NEIGHBOURING ACCOMMODATION DOORS**

>
> **THE LESSON IN THE NUMBERS**
> *   A reserved *bidang usaha* is closed to every PMA, big or small. Your modest size does not exempt you; your PMA classification condemns you.
> *   A PMA is *always* a large enterprise in law. The Annex II reserved lines are off-limits by definition, not by moratorium.

**4. The Money**

The coliving build is heavy—a compound lease or purchase, twelve rooms of fit-out, a workspace, a kitchen, a pool, plus the minimum paid-up capital every PMA must commit. But unlike the café (where the money was ready and only the code was blocked), the coliving founder faces a problem money genuinely cannot solve, because the barrier is a reservation, not a price. You could arrive with ten million dollars and still not be able to take the guest-house scope of 55209 as a PMA, because the obstacle is a line the Perpres reserves for local small enterprise, not your bank balance.

This is what makes the UMKM-scale trap so brutal: there is no amount of capital that converts a Besar-classified PMA into the small enterprise the code requires. The only real path into this kind of small accommodation is to *change the structure of the lodging*—and the honest options are narrow. The genuinely open accommodation codes are the *hotels* (55101–55106), which carry their own scope conditions and imply a far larger, more formal operation than a twelve-room surf compound. Scaling *up* into a clearly-registrable hotel category, partnering with a licensed local accommodation provider, or separating the business so the PMA owns only the open coworking/community/education layer while the beds sit with a properly-licensed partner—these are the real moves. None of them is the simple "register my little compound" Finn walked in imagining.

The classic trap—*set up a local nominee to hold the UMKM accommodation while I control it informally*—is the nominee structure, and it is exactly the arrangement the crackdown is designed to expose. Building a heavy, visible compound on a nominee foundation is among the riskiest bets a foreigner can make on the island. (The math of that bet has its own chapter; the short version is: don't.)

**5. The Timeline**

For the guest-house scope of 55209 under a PMA, there is no timeline—not because the province is slow, but because the first step (a PMA taking a *bidang usaha* the Perpres reserves for local small enterprise) is legally impossible. You cannot schedule what the reservation forbids. Finn's real timeline questions were architectural: Do I scale the vision up into a genuine hotel category (55101–55106) that a PMA *can* register—accepting a bigger, more formal, more capital-intensive build than the intimate compound I imagined? Do I keep the PMA to the open layers (community, programs, coworking if it survives) and partner out the beds entirely? Or do I accept that "small foreign-owned coliving compound" is, as a clean legal structure, simply not a thing the law currently allows?

These are not "how long" questions. They are "what is this business actually allowed to be" questions—and the surf-coliving dream, more than most, forces the founder to choose between scaling up into legality or scaling the ambition down to a program-and-partnership model.

**6. The Bali Angle**

The UMKM reservation is, of all the obstacles in this book, the most explicitly redistributive—and the most revealing of the island's intent. Homestays, small guesthouses, villas, coliving: these are precisely the businesses a Balinese family can realistically build and run. Reserving them for UMKM (and thus for Indonesians, since a foreigner's only vehicle is the large-by-law PMA) is the province and the national framework working in concert to keep the small-accommodation economy—the most accessible rung on the tourism ladder—in local hands.

For Finn, the Bali Angle reframes the rejection entirely. The island is not telling him surfing and community are unwelcome. It is telling him that *being the small landlord*—owning the modest rooms that a local family could own—is the one role reserved for Balinese. The surf, the lineup, the community, the work: all of that he can build. The beds, at small scale, belong to the island's own people. The most respectful and durable version of his dream is one that contributes to that economy rather than displacing it—owning the experience and the community while the lodging stays in local hands. Read that way, the scale law isn't an insult to his ambition; it's a map to the version of the dream the island will actually let him keep.

**7. The Pitfall**

The pitfall that nearly cost Finn a locked-in lease was the most invisible trap in this entire book: the belief that *small means simple*. He reasoned that a modest twelve-room compound was the *easiest* possible accommodation business—too small to attract scrutiny, too humble to be the kind of thing regulations target. He had it exactly backwards. His smallness was the problem: the codes that fit a small operation are the very ones reserved for UMKM, and his PMA—large by law—was locked out of them precisely *because* they're meant for the small.

The second pitfall is the bundling trap he shared with the retreat founder: treating "surf-coliving" as one business when it is at least two codes (lodging + coworking), each with its own ugly verdict, neither of which is a clean open door. "It's a coliving business" is not a registrable statement; it's a marketing phrase covering a stack of separate legal questions.

The crucial lesson: in Bali, your *legal classification* can disqualify you from a business no matter how small or well-funded you are. A PMA is always a large enterprise, and large enterprises are barred from the reserved lines by definition—not by moratorium, not by risk class, but by an explicit allocation in the investment rules. Before you fall for the intimacy of a small accommodation dream, ask the question that has nothing to do with money or charm: *is this line of business allocated to local small enterprise in the Perpres annex?* If it is, the door was never open to you, and the only way through is to change the shape of the dream—up into a hotel category, or sideways into owning the experience while the island keeps the beds.

**8. The Way Through**

Finn came in wanting to own a building full of beds. The law says the beds, at that scale, belong to a Balinese family — not to his PMA. That door is structural, and no amount of capital reopens it. But the *dream* was never really the beds. The dream was the dawn lineup, the morning of deep work, the kitchen full of people who became friends, the program that turned a week in Pererenan into the best month of someone's year. Strip the lodging out and ask what Finn's PMA can legally *own*, and a real business is still standing — it just owns the experience instead of the real estate. Here is where the surf-coliving founder actually has room to move.

> ### THE OPEN ROUTES — CANDIDATE ALTERNATIVES (VERIFY BEFORE YOU RELY)
>
> *Each code below carries a ground-truth status of "registrable for a PMA, risk/scope-conditional" — not a clean green light. "Registrable" is not "permit-free" and is not "approved sight-unseen": every one carries its own sector licensing, and its actual OSS risk grade and any Bali-specific scope conditions must be tested in the live system before you build against it. None of them lets your PMA own the beds. Together they let it own everything around the beds — once each is cleared on its own terms.*
>
> **ROUTE 1 — Own the surf program, not the lodging (the PMA's true core).**
> **93297 — Wisata Tirta (water tourism)** is the honest home for the thing Finn is genuinely best at: organised surf experiences, coaching, dawn-and-dusk guided sessions, board-and-water programs. Pair it with **85510 — Pendidikan Olahraga dan Rekreasi (sport & recreation education)** for the structured-instruction / surf-school side. *Caveat — and this one is load-bearing:* both codes are "registrable but conditional." Each is a licensed activity with its own sector requirements (tourism registration, instructor/safety standards, often a tourism business certification), AND the live OSS risk grade for each must be confirmed before you commit — a low or medium-low risk grade can trigger Bali-specific scope blocks that the headline "open" status does not show. Direct system testing on the live platform is mandatory for both, not optional. Neither code authorises you to sell a bed for the night.
>
> **ROUTE 2 — Be the program operator, sell the stay as a packaged experience.**
> **79121 — Aktivitas Biro Perjalanan Wisata (travel agency / tour operator)** is the legitimate vehicle for packaging a "surf-and-work week" — the itinerary, the bookings, the program, the community — while the actual nights are supplied by a properly-licensed accommodation provider you contract with. Of the open routes this is among the more robustly registrable for a PMA in Bali, but it is still scope-conditional: a travel-bureau licence lets you *sell and assemble* the experience; it does not let you secretly *be* the unlicensed hotel. The accommodation in the package must sit with a partner who can lawfully provide it.
>
> **ROUTE 3 — Own the gathering place: food, community, the daytime hub.**
> **56101 — Aktivitas Penyediaan Makanan di Bangunan Tetap (food service in a fixed building)** lets the compound's kitchen and communal table become a real, owned business — the café-restaurant heart of the community even when the rooms belong to someone else. Like Route 2 it is among the more solidly registrable open codes for a PMA, but it is not unconditional: F&B carries its own scope conditions (food-safety / higher-risk obligations), and a restaurant licence is a restaurant licence — it grants no right to serve as accommodation, and no alcohol entitlement on its own.
>
> **ROUTE 4 — The partner structure (no single code — a shape).**
> Split the business at the seam §2 already drew. The **PMA owns the brand, the program, the surf school, the community and the food** (Routes 1–3, each cleared on its own terms). A **properly-licensed local UMKM partner owns the rooms** and provides the lodging under its own UMKM-scale registration — the one role the island reserves for its own people. This is the *opposite* of a nominee: the local entity genuinely owns and operates the beds, on its own licence, at the scale the law intends; your PMA contracts with it arm's-length for guests. *Caveat:* it only works if the local partner is real — real ownership, real control of the lodging, real licence. A "partner" who is a foreigner's puppet is a nominee with extra paperwork (see below).
>
> **ROUTE 5 — Scale the ambition up, not the workaround.**
> The genuinely large, formal accommodation categories — the star and non-star *hotels* (55101–55106) — are a different animal from a twelve-room compound, and even they are scope-gated in Bali (status "open but scope-dependent," not a clean green light). This is an honest path *only* if Finn is willing to become a real hotel operator: a bigger, more formal, more heavily-licensed build than the intimate surf house he imagined. *Caveat:* do not treat a hotel category as a back-door for the same small compound — that is the code-mismatch trap (§7) wearing a bigger jacket. Scaling up means actually scaling up, scope clearance and all.

**WHAT IS NOT A ROUTE**

- **The nominee.** A local "owner" who is really you, controlling UMKM beds informally, is exactly the structure the crackdown was built to expose — and a heavy, visible compound is the easiest possible target. Routes 1–4 exist *so you never have to*.
- **The code-mismatch.** Registering 93297 or 79121 or 56101 and then quietly running a twelve-room lodging house underneath it is the same trap §7 warns about: running activity Z under code Y. A surf-tourism licence is not an accommodation licence; a restaurant licence is not a hotel; a tour-operator licence is not the right to keep guests overnight on your own books.
- **"Add a code and the licence upgrades itself."** Stacking secondary KBLI does not auto-grant anything. Each open activity above unlocks *only its own* activity, still needs its own sector permit, and still has to clear its own live risk/scope check before you can lawfully run it. Nothing here dissolves the UMKM reservation — it routes *around* the beds, it does not erase the rule, and none of it is approved until you have tested each code's live status.

The principle Finn has to internalise is the one the island has been signalling all along: **stop trying to own the part that's reserved, and own the part that's yours.** The beds, at small scale, are Balinese by design — that is not negotiable and not worth fighting. But the surf, the school, the program, the table, the brand, the community — every magnetic thing that made his vision more than a guesthouse — those are all activities a PMA *can* legally hold, once each is cleared on its own terms. The durable version of the surf-coliving dream is not a foreigner quietly owning a compound full of rooms. It is a foreigner owning the *reason people come*, partnered with the locals who own the place they sleep. That is not a downgrade of the dream. It is the only version of it the island will let him keep — and the only one he can sleep soundly inside.

*Before you sign anything, scan to check the live status of KBLI 55209 against the candidate open routes — 93297 (water tourism), 85510 (sport & recreation education), 79121 (travel agency) and 56101 (food service) — and confirm, for each, both the sector permits it demands and its live OSS risk grade and Bali scope conditions, on the Nuzantara platform.*
