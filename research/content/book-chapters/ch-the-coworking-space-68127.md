# CHAPTER: The Coworking Space

**1. The Dream**

Daniel had built three startups and sold two of them, and somewhere in the wreckage and triumph of all that he had fallen in love not with any product but with the *room*—the specific energy of a good coworking space at ten in the morning, when forty laptops are open and the air hums with the particular electricity of people building things. He had spent a decade renting desks in those rooms across Berlin, Lisbon, and Chiang Mai. Now he wanted to own one.

And he wanted to own it in Canggu, which had become, without anyone quite deciding it, the digital-nomad capital of the planet. The math was intoxicating. Thousands of remote workers arriving every month, each one desperate for reliable wifi, air conditioning, fast internet, a community, and a desk that wasn't a café table with a dying laptop battery. Daniel had seen the figures: a well-run coworking space in Bali could clear tens of thousands of dollars a month, especially stacked with event hosting, private offices, and memberships.

His vision was beautiful and specific. A converted warehouse in Berawa. Polished concrete and tropical plants. Phone booths and hot desks and a rooftop for sunset networking. A members' Slack, a wellness corner, a barista in the lobby. He would build the room he had spent his whole career renting—the engine of the nomad economy, owned at last by someone who understood it from the inside.

He walked into the consultant's office with a pitch deck. He pitched it the way he'd pitched VCs. "Coworking. Canggu. I want to register the PMA this month. What's the code—68127, the office-building management one?"

**2. The Reality Check**

The consultant let him finish the deck. It was a good deck. Then he set it down gently.

"You've got the right code," he said. "68127, *Pengelolaan Gedung Perkantoran*—management of office buildings, which is exactly where coworking lives. And nationally it's one hundred percent open to foreign ownership. So far your deck is correct."

"I hear a 'but' coming."

"But in Bali, 68127 is flagged *proposed for closure* to foreign PMA. It sits in the real-estate cluster the province has explicitly targeted in the moratorium wave. It is not in the same final-closed state as the café down the road—it is in a more dangerous limbo: a door the province has publicly signaled it intends to shut. Building a capital-heavy coworking PMA on a code marked 'proposed for closure' is like signing a ten-year lease on a building with a demolition notice taped to the door."

Daniel frowned. "Proposed isn't closed. Maybe it never closes."

"Maybe. And maybe it closes the month after you've sunk three hundred thousand dollars into a warehouse fit-out. That is the precise risk you'd be taking. With a fully blocked code, at least the answer is a clean 'no' and you walk away with your capital intact. With a 'proposed for closure' code, the temptation is to race the regulation—to register and build fast, before the door shuts. People do it. Some win. But you'd be betting a very large, very physical, very immovable investment on the timing of a provincial decision you don't control."

"Why is coworking even on the list? It's the engine of the whole Canggu economy."

"That's exactly *why*," the consultant said. "Look at it from the province's chair. Coworking is real-estate management dressed in startup clothes—a foreigner owning and monetizing Balinese building space, capturing the rent of the nomad boom. Real estate is the single most sensitive sector on the island, and the province is moving to keep building-management and space-letting in local hands. The fact that your room hums with creative energy doesn't change what it is on the OSS form: a foreigner running Balinese real estate. That is the thing the province is most determined to slow down."

**4. The Money**

This is the chapter where the money matters most, because coworking is *capital-heavy* and *immovable*—the worst possible combination to pair with an uncertain code. A coworking build is a major commitment: a long commercial lease or building purchase, a substantial fit-out (electrical, networking, HVAC, furniture, phone booths, kitchen), plus the minimum paid-up capital every PMA must commit and the working capital to survive the months before memberships ramp. This is not a laptop business. It is bricks, cables, and concrete.

Now overlay that against a "proposed for closure" code, and the risk becomes vivid. Every dollar Daniel would spend is poured into fixed, location-locked infrastructure that cannot be packed up and moved if the province finalizes the closure mid-build. With a weightless business, an unfavorable ruling costs you a company formation. With coworking, it can cost you a warehouse. The financial exposure is not just large—it is *stranded*. You cannot recover a fit-out the way you can recover an idea.

The workaround the clever founder reaches for—*structure it as a "services" or "community" business instead of real-estate management*—is exactly the kind of code-mislabeling the authorities are watching for. If the substance of your business is letting desks and office space, calling it something else on the form does not change what it is. It just adds enforcement risk on top of the closure risk.

**5. The Timeline**

A coworking timeline is long even in the best case—lease or purchase, building permits, fit-out, licensing, then the slow ramp to occupancy. But for 68127 in Bali, the timeline carries a second clock that Daniel does not control: the regulatory countdown to closure. He would be running two timelines at once—his build schedule and the province's closure schedule—and racing to finish the first before the second ends.

That is the worst kind of timeline: a race against a clock you can't see, with a heavy, immovable bet on the line. A blocked code has no timeline (nothing to schedule) but also no false hope. A "proposed for closure" code has a timeline that *tempts*—it whispers "go fast, you might make it"—and that whisper is how founders end up with capital stranded in a half-built space the province decides to close. The honest timeline question for Daniel is not "how fast can I build?" but "am I willing to bet a warehouse on out-running a provincial letter?"

**6. The Bali Angle**

Coworking sits on the rawest nerve in the whole Bali-foreigner relationship: real estate. Of everything the province is anxious about, foreign control of Balinese land and buildings is the deepest fear—and a coworking space is, beneath the plants and the espresso, a foreigner monetizing Balinese building space at scale. It captures the rent of the nomad economy and routes it to a foreign owner. From the island's side of the table, that is precisely the dynamic the moratorium exists to slow.

The deeper Bali Angle is that coworking embodies the exact thing the province resents about the nomad boom: foreigners extracting value from the island's desirability—its wifi-and-sunset appeal—while the underlying asset (the land, the building) and the surrounding community see comparatively little. Reserving office-real-estate management for local ownership is the province's attempt to keep the rent of the boom in Balinese hands. Daniel's beautiful room full of humming laptops is, from Denpasar's chair, the symbol of the very imbalance the moratorium was written to correct. Reading 68127's "proposed for closure" status as a policy signal—rather than a bureaucratic accident—is the only way to make a sane decision about whether to bet on it.

**7. The Pitfall**

The pitfall that could cost Daniel a warehouse was the costume—the seductive belief that coworking is a *tech* or *community* business rather than a *real-estate* business. He thought in terms of Slack channels and member events and startup energy. The OSS system thinks in terms of who controls the building and collects the rent. The gap between how the founder sees his business and how the law classifies it is exactly where the money disappears.

The second, sharper pitfall is "proposed" optimism: the belief that because the door is not yet fully closed, it's safe to charge through it. "Proposed for closure" is not "open." It is a warning that the door is moving, and the heaviest, most immovable investments are the worst candidates for racing a closing door. A consultant whose code is merely "proposed for closure" can pivot cheaply. A coworking founder mid-fit-out cannot.

The crucial lesson: match the *weight* of your investment to the *certainty* of your code. The heavier and more location-locked the business, the more you need a code that is *firmly* open—not nationally open, not provincially tolerated, not "proposed for closure," but solidly, durably open in this province. Daniel understood the nomad economy better than almost anyone. What he nearly missed is that in Bali, a coworking space is not a tech bet—it is a real-estate bet, on the most contested ground on the island, under a code with a demolition notice on the door. The dream is real. But you do not build something this heavy on a foundation this uncertain.
