# Indonesia Visa Comparisons: Side-by-Side for Every Common Decision (2026)

The Indonesia visa system has over thirty distinct permit categories. Most people, however, do not need to understand all of them — they need to understand one comparison: the two or three options that apply to their specific situation and the criteria that separate them. Coming to Bali twice a year as a tourist? One comparison. Planning to work remotely for a foreign company? A different one. Ready to convert five years of KITAS into permanent residency? Another one entirely.

This hub covers the four comparisons that resolve the majority of Indonesia visa questions. All permit codes use 2026 nomenclature as established by Permenkumham 22/2023 and UU 63/2024.

## C1 vs D1 — Single Entry vs Multiple Entry Tourism {#c1-vs-d1}

The C1 is a single-entry tourist visa valid for 60 days with one extension to 180 days. The D1 is a multiple-entry tourist visa that allows stays of up to 60 days per entry across a validity window of one, two, or five years. For travelers visiting Indonesia once per year, the C1 is simpler and cheaper. For anyone visiting two or more times in a calendar year, the D1 almost always works out less expensive in total — and eliminates the administrative effort of reapplying each trip. The most common mistake is buying a C1 on every visit out of habit, without ever calculating whether the D1 would cost less over twelve months. If you have made more than two visa-on-arrival entries in the past year, run the numbers.

## C2 vs D2 — Single Entry vs Multiple Entry Business {#c2-vs-d2}

The C2 is a single-entry Business Visit Visa suited to one-off engagements: a product launch, a supplier audit, a contract signing trip. The D2 is its multiple-entry equivalent, designed for foreign nationals who meet Indonesian clients or partners on a recurring basis throughout the year. Neither the C2 nor the D2 authorises working for an Indonesian company or receiving local income — they are visit permits, not work authorisations. The distinction matters: consultants who believe their D2 permits them to invoice Indonesian clients are operating outside its conditions. If your engagement with Indonesia has evolved from occasional visits to ongoing deliverables paid by a local entity, the correct step is conversion to an E-category permit, not a D2 renewal.

## E23 vs E28A vs E33G — Worker, Investor, or Digital Nomad {#e23-vs-e28a-vs-e33g}

This is the comparison most frequently answered incorrectly — including by some immigration consultants. The **E23** is a Working KITAS for foreign nationals employed by an Indonesian entity: your employer sponsors you, obtains a RPTKA approval, and you work in Indonesia for that employer. The **E28A** is an Investor KITAS for shareholders of a PT PMA — you hold equity in a locally established foreign investment company and reside in Indonesia as an owner. The **E33G** is the Digital Nomad permit (_Second Home / ITAS Rumah Kedua_), designed for remote workers who are employed by or contracted to a company **outside** Indonesia and earn income from that foreign source exclusively. The E33G is regularly mislabelled as an "investor visa" on expat forums — this is incorrect. It has no equity requirement and no Indonesian employer. Choosing between these three based on lifestyle rather than legal structure is the most expensive visa mistake in Bali.

## KITAS vs KITAP — Limited vs Permanent Stay {#kitas-vs-kitap}

KITAS (_Kartu Izin Tinggal Terbatas_) is a limited stay permit, typically valid for one or two years and renewable. KITAP (_Kartu Izin Tinggal Tetap_) is permanent residency — it does not expire and does not require renewal cycles. KITAP is available to foreign nationals who have held a KITAS continuously for at least five years without a permit gap, and who meet additional integration requirements including language proficiency and evidence of Indonesian community ties. KITAP is not automatic: you must actively apply, and the application is subject to discretionary review by Ditjen Imigrasi. Many long-term residents assume they qualify after five years without checking whether their KITAS history has any gaps — a single missed renewal that lapsed even by a few days can reset the clock.
