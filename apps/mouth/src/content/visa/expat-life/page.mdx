## Taxes & Tax Residency {#taxes--tax-residency}

Indonesia uses a 183-day rule: if you are physically present in the country for more than 183 days within any 12-month period, you become a tax resident and are subject to _Pajak Penghasilan_ (PPh) on your worldwide income — not just what you earn locally. This applies regardless of your visa type. Holding an E33G Digital Nomad permit does not make you tax-exempt; it only governs your right to stay. Many expats assume their visa category determines their tax status — it does not. Separately, an NPWP (_Nomor Pokok Wajib Pajak_, tax identification number) is required for anyone earning in Indonesia and is increasingly requested when opening bank accounts or signing lease agreements, even when local income is zero.

## Banking for Expats {#banking-for-expats}

You can open a standard Indonesian savings account — but only with an active KITAS. Tourist visas, visa-on-arrival, and the C1 single-entry visa are not accepted as residency documents by any major Indonesian bank. The most expat-friendly options tend to be BCA, Bank Mandiri, and CIMB Niaga, each of which has English-speaking staff at branches in Seminyak, Canggu, and SCBD Jakarta. For outbound transfers and remittances, Bank Indonesia reporting requirements apply above certain thresholds — your relationship manager should explain these at account opening, but many do not. The practical sequence: secure your KITAS first, then open a bank account. Attempting it the other way around is the single most common banking frustration among new arrivals.

## International Schools {#international-schools}

Bali's international school cluster is concentrated around Canggu, Seminyak, and Sanur, with a smaller presence in Ubud. Curricula on offer include IB (_International Baccalaureate_), Cambridge IGCSE, Australian, and American. Annual fees range from approximately USD 8,000 for entry-level programs to USD 25,000 and above for full IB or Cambridge tracks at established schools. The enrollment timing is the part that blindsides most families: enrollment windows for the following academic year typically open six to nine months in advance, and popular schools fill their places early. Arriving in August — the start of most academic years — and expecting to enroll immediately is a reliable way to spend the first semester on a waiting list. If you are moving with school-age children, start the enrollment process before you move.

## Healthcare & Insurance {#healthcare--insurance}

Expats with an active KITAS are eligible to enroll in _BPJS Kesehatan_, Indonesia's national health insurance scheme. BPJS covers a broad range of services at very low cost, but it operates on a referral-and-queue system: you start at a _puskesmas_ (community clinic), get referred upward if needed, and wait. For routine care this works. For anything time-sensitive, specialist-heavy, or involving a pre-existing condition, it is not sufficient on its own. Private health insurance — from regional providers such as AXA Mandiri, Allianz, or Pacific Cross — covers direct hospital access, private rooms, and critically, medical evacuation, which BPJS does not. In Bali, BIMC Hospital (Kuta and Nusa Dua), Siloam Hospitals, and Kasih Ibu are the primary facilities used by expats and equipped for international-standard care.

## The Right Order Matters

All of this feels like a lot — and it is, if you try to do it all at once without a sequence. The order that works: secure the right visa first, then activate your KITAS, then register for an NPWP, then open a bank account, then arrange insurance, then sort schooling. Each step unlocks the next. The reason the right visa matters here is that your KITAS category directly affects what banking products you can access and how your tax residency is assessed.
