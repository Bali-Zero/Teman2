## The Facts

The number of Americans exploring permanent relocation abroad has been climbing steadily, driven by a convergence of factors: rising cost of living, political polarisation, healthcare costs, and the normalisation of remote work. For many, what began as a pandemic-era experiment living overseas has matured into a serious life decision.

The financial calculus is complex. US citizens are among the only nationals in the world subject to citizenship-based taxation, meaning they owe the IRS regardless of where they live. Renouncing US citizenship — the only way to fully sever tax obligations — is a serious and largely irreversible step that requires filing a final tax return, potentially paying an exit tax on unrealised gains, and surrendering a passport that grants visa-free access to 186 countries.

For those who want to live abroad without renouncing, the Foreign Earned Income Exclusion (FEIE) allows qualifying Americans to exclude a portion of foreign-earned income from US taxation — up to roughly $126,500 in 2024 — but investment income, rental income, and Social Security remain taxable. The Foreign Tax Credit offers partial relief, but structuring finances to minimise double taxation requires specialist advice.

Legal residency in a destination country is a separate challenge. Most nations offer a spectrum of options: tourist visas (typically 30–180 days), long-term retirement visas, investor visas, and work permits tied to local employment or company ownership. The stability and scope of these pathways vary dramatically by country and can change with little notice.

Beyond legal and financial logistics, permanent relocation involves healthcare access, estate planning across jurisdictions, schooling for dependents, banking (US banks routinely close accounts of overseas residents due to FATCA compliance costs), and the psychological weight of severing social and community ties. Those who approach the decision methodically — spending extended time in the destination, consulting specialists, and stress-testing their assumptions — report significantly better outcomes than those who move impulsively.
