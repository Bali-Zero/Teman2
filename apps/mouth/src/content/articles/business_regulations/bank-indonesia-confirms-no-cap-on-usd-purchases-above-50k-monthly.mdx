## The Facts

Bank Indonesia (BI), the country's central bank, has moved to address widespread confusion in the market by formally clarifying the scope of its foreign exchange transaction regulations. The clarification confirms that Indonesian banking regulations do not impose a hard ceiling on US dollar purchases — but they do introduce a tiered compliance framework once monthly transaction volumes cross the USD 50,000 threshold.

Under the existing framework, any individual or legal entity seeking to purchase foreign currency equivalent to or exceeding USD 50,000 per month must provide supporting documentation to the executing bank. This documentation is intended to demonstrate the underlying economic purpose of the transaction — such as trade invoices, service contracts, offshore loan repayments, dividend distributions, or investment transfers. Banks are required to verify and retain these documents as part of their Know Your Customer and anti-money laundering obligations.

The clarification appears to be a direct response to reports from banking customers — particularly foreign nationals and PT PMA (foreign-owned limited liability company) operators — who had been informed by branch-level bank staff that a USD 50,000 monthly purchase limit was in force. Bank Indonesia has confirmed this interpretation is incorrect: the threshold triggers documentation requirements, not a prohibition on further purchases.

This distinction is significant within the regulatory context of Indonesia's 2019 Foreign Exchange Traffic Law (UU No. 24/1999) and subsequent BI regulations, which grant the central bank broad authority to monitor and regulate cross-border capital flows without necessarily restricting them. The policy goal is transparency and macroeconomic stability, not capital controls in the conventional sense.

Bank Indonesia has not announced new rules in conjunction with this clarification — the underlying regulations remain unchanged. However, the public communication signals that BI is aware of inconsistent implementation at the commercial bank level and is taking steps to standardize interpretation across the banking sector. Businesses and individuals who have been turned away or delayed due to bank staff misreading the threshold as a hard limit now have formal grounds to challenge such decisions.
