## The Facts

Indonesia has formalised visa-free entry arrangements with three new partner countries — Lebanon, Saint Vincent and the Grenadines, and the Dominican Republic — as part of a diplomatic offensive conducted on the sidelines of the 81st United Nations General Assembly in New York. The agreements were signed on September 23, 2026, by Indonesian Foreign Minister Sugiono and are limited in their current scope to holders of diplomatic and service passports, a category of arrangement Indonesia designates as PBVDD (Perjanjian Bebas Visa Diplomatik dan Dinas).

The Indonesian Foreign Ministry confirmed the signings in an official statement issued on September 24, 2026, describing them as 'a concrete step to strengthen bilateral relations and increase interaction between Indonesia and the three countries, while opening opportunities for broader cooperation.' The ministry stated that Indonesia views the PBVDD framework as a first phase, with Jakarta publicly expressing the intent to eventually expand visa-free facilities to ordinary citizens travelling between Indonesia and all three countries.

The agreement with Lebanon carries an additional diplomatic dimension. On the same occasion, Foreign Minister Sugiono held bilateral talks with Lebanese Foreign Minister and Emigrants Yousef Raggi, covering economic cooperation, development assistance, and regional security — with emphasis on the situation in Palestine. Indonesia has positioned the Palestinian issue as a central plank of its foreign policy at this year's General Assembly, consistent with its long-standing advocacy for Palestinian self-determination at the multilateral level.

The inclusion of Saint Vincent and the Grenadines and the Dominican Republic reflects Indonesia's continued effort to deepen ties with the Caribbean basin, a region where Indonesian diplomatic presence has historically been modest. Both small island states are members of the CARICOM grouping, and the agreements signal Jakarta's intent to cultivate bilateral relationships across regions that are not traditional partners — a pattern consistent with the Prabowo administration's broader strategy of expanding Indonesia's global diplomatic footprint.

As of the time of publication, no implementation timeline or formal entry-into-force date has been announced for any of the three agreements. The specific terms — including maximum permitted stay, eligible travel document specifications, and reciprocal conditions — have not been publicly disclosed. The agreements are expected to require domestic ratification procedures in both signatory states before they become operative.

## In Practice

For the overwhelming majority of foreigners living and working in Bali, these agreements have no immediate effect on daily life or business operations. The PBVDD framework applies exclusively to diplomatic and service passport holders — accredited diplomats and government officials — not entrepreneurs, digital nomads, retirees, property investors, or business travellers on ordinary passports.

Nationals of Lebanon, Saint Vincent and the Grenadines, and the Dominican Republic holding standard passports should continue to use existing Indonesian entry pathways: Visa on Arrival for eligible nationalities, the B211A social-cultural visa, or the appropriate KITAS category for longer stays. No change to port-of-entry procedures at Ngurah Rai or elsewhere has been announced, and none should be assumed until an official entry-into-force notification is published.

The broader relevance is directional. Indonesia's commitment to expanding these deals to ordinary citizens — stated explicitly by the Foreign Ministry — means those with business interests linking Bali to Lebanon, the Dominican Republic, or the Eastern Caribbean should monitor this space. Bilateral economic cooperation frameworks tend to open procurement, trade mission, and joint-venture opportunities that are easier to access once diplomatic groundwork is laid.
