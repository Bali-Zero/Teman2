## The Facts

The L-1 visa is a nonimmigrant classification issued by US Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) that allows multinational companies to transfer qualifying employees from a foreign office to a related US entity. It comes in two categories: L-1A, for managers and executives, and L-1B, for employees with specialized knowledge. The distinction is significant — L-1A holders can use their status as a direct pathway to EB-1C green card eligibility, one of the most efficient routes to permanent residency available to corporate transferees.

To qualify, a petitioner must demonstrate a qualifying corporate relationship — meaning the US employer and the foreign employer must be the same entity, a parent, branch, subsidiary, or affiliate. The employee must have worked for the qualifying organization abroad for at least one continuous year within the three years preceding the petition, and must be coming to the US to work in a managerial, executive, or specialized knowledge capacity.

Approval rates for L-1A petitions have historically been stronger than L-1B, largely because 'specialized knowledge' is a more subjective and contested standard. USCIS has issued Requests for Evidence (RFEs) on L-1B petitions at elevated rates over the past several years, with adjudicators scrutinizing whether the claimed knowledge is truly proprietary or advanced versus general industry knowledge. In 2025, overall L-1 approval rates hovered in the 75–85% range depending on employer size and petition type, with blanket L-1 petitions filed by large multinationals enjoying significantly higher approval rates than individual petitions from smaller companies.

Processing times in 2026 remain bifurcated. Standard processing can run three to six months for form I-129 petitions, while premium processing — available for an additional USCIS fee — guarantees a 15-business-day adjudication window. For new office L-1 petitions, where a foreign company is establishing a US presence for the first time, USCIS grants an initial one-year validity period rather than the standard three years, requiring early re-filing to maintain status.

The L-1 does not carry a numerical cap, unlike the H-1B, meaning there is no annual lottery and petitions can be filed at any time of year. Spouses of L-1 holders receive L-2 status and are authorized to work in the US, an increasingly valuable feature for dual-career couples navigating international mobility.
