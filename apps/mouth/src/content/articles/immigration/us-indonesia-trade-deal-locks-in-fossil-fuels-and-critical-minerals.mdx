## The Facts

The United States and Indonesia have reached a bilateral trade agreement that prioritises American access to Indonesian fossil fuel exports alongside a framework for securing critical minerals essential to the global clean energy transition. The deal, reported by The Independent, represents one of the more significant economic arrangements between Washington and Jakarta in recent years.

Indonesia holds some of the world's largest reserves of nickel, cobalt, and bauxite — materials in heavy demand for electric vehicle batteries, semiconductors, and renewable energy infrastructure. The agreement is understood to give American companies preferential or structured access to these resources, potentially countering China's dominant position in the global critical minerals supply chain.

On the fossil fuel side, the deal secures continued flows of Indonesian coal and other hydrocarbons to U.S. markets or U.S.-aligned buyers, a notable concession given ongoing international pressure on Indonesia to accelerate its energy transition away from coal dependency. Indonesia is among the world's top coal exporters, and coal revenues remain a significant pillar of state income.

The timing of the agreement coincides with intensifying great-power competition over resource access across Southeast Asia. Washington has been actively courting ASEAN nations as part of a broader economic strategy to diversify supply chains away from Chinese dominance. Indonesia, as the region's largest economy and a G20 member, occupies a pivotal position in that calculus.

The agreement's specific terms — including tariff structures, investment protections, royalty arrangements, and environmental carve-outs — had not been fully disclosed in initial reporting. Indonesian officials have previously signalled a desire to add value domestically before exporting raw minerals, a policy stance that has created friction with trading partners seeking unprocessed ore.
