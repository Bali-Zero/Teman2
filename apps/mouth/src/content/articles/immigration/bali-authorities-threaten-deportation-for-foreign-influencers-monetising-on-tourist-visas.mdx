## The Facts

Indonesian immigration authorities in Bali have moved to enforce a stricter interpretation of visa-permitted activities, placing foreign social media influencers squarely in the crosshairs of deportation proceedings. Officials have made clear that earning income — directly or indirectly — from content produced on Indonesian soil while holding a tourist or visit visa constitutes an unauthorised work activity under Indonesian immigration law.

The enforcement posture reflects a broader national policy shift. Indonesia's Immigration Law No. 6 of 2011 prohibits holders of visit visas from conducting activities that generate income or that could be deemed employment. For years, this prohibition was applied primarily to traditional employment — foreigners caught teaching, waiting tables, or managing properties. Authorities are now extending the same legal logic to digital labour: a foreign national who films a sponsored reel on a Bali rice terrace and receives payment from a brand is, in their reading, performing undeclared work.

Bali has become a particularly sensitive jurisdiction. The island receives a disproportionate share of Indonesia's foreign arrivals, and local immigration officials have reported a surge in 'digital nomads' and influencers who use the island as a content backdrop while deriving income abroad. The Directorate General of Immigration has the authority under Article 75 of Law 6/2011 to impose 'selective policy' deportations against foreigners deemed to have abused their immigration status, without requiring a criminal conviction.

Deportation in these cases typically follows a process of detection — often triggered by publicly visible commercial content, tip-offs, or routine checks at co-working spaces — followed by a summons, administrative review, and an order to depart. Subjects may also be placed on a re-entry blacklist (cekal), barring future visits for a period determined by the severity of the violation.

The crackdown is not limited to influencers alone. Immigration officials have signalled that any foreigner conducting business activities — including remote work for foreign employers — without the appropriate visa and work permit (IMTA) is potentially in violation. The influencer category has attracted particular public and regulatory attention because the commercial nature of the activity is self-documented on social platforms, making enforcement relatively straightforward compared to detecting covert employment.
