**Most expats think 183 days is the only trigger for Indonesian tax residency. It is not. Indonesia applies a dual-test — and getting this wrong can mean unexpected worldwide income tax liability.**

---

## The Facts

Indonesian tax residency is governed by the Income Tax Law (UU PPh No. 36/2008), specifically Pasal 2 ayat 3. Under this law, an individual is considered a domestic tax subject — and therefore a tax resident — if they meet **either** of the following criteria:

**Criterion 1 — The 183-Day Rule**
The individual is physically present in Indonesia for more than 183 days within any 12-month period. These days do not need to be consecutive.

**Criterion 2 — Intent to Reside**
The individual is present in Indonesia during the tax year **and** demonstrates an intent to reside in the country. Intent is assessed through objective indicators including:

- Holding a valid KITAS or KITAP
- Having immediate family (spouse, children) residing in Indonesia
- Maintaining a business presence or active PT PMA
- Owning or long-term leasing a property in Indonesia

This is the part most expats miss. You do not need to cross the 183-day threshold if the DJP determines you have clear intent to make Indonesia your base.

---

## What This Means in Practice

**KITAS Investor (E28A) holders**
If you hold an E28A Investor KITAS and have an active PT PMA, family in Bali, and a leased property — you may be treated as a tax resident from day one, regardless of how many days you have spent in Indonesia this year. The intent indicators are strong enough to trigger residency independently.

**Digital Nomad E33G holders**
Conversely, an E33G holder who has spent 200 days in Indonesia but maintains no family presence, no business entity, and no long-term lease — and who formally declares no intent to reside — may still be able to argue non-resident status. However, this requires careful documentation and is not automatic. The DJP has not issued explicit implementing regulations confirming E33G holders are categorically exempt from tax residency rules.

**PT PMA owners living abroad**
If you own a PT PMA but split your time between Bali and your home country, your residency status depends on which side of the dual-test you fall on. The existence of a PT PMA alone is one intent indicator — but combined with other factors, it can push you firmly into resident status.

## Bali Zero Take

### The Hidden Insight

The 183-day rule is a hard floor, not a ceiling. The DJP can establish residency below 183 days if the intent factors are present. This is a statutory provision, not an administrative interpretation — it is written directly into UU PPh Pasal 2 ayat 3 huruf b.

### Our Analysis

The practical implication is this: if you are building a life in Bali — visa, family, business, property — count yourself as a tax resident and plan accordingly. Attempting to manage day-counts without addressing the intent criteria is a half-measure that creates exposure.

The E33G visa is designed to attract remote workers with favorable conditions, and the government has signaled positive intent toward digital nomads. But until explicit implementing regulations confirm a statutory carve-out from tax residency, conservative planning is the appropriate stance for anyone spending significant time in Indonesia on this visa.

### Our Advice

Do the dual-test assessment before the tax year ends, not after. If you are a tax resident, register for an NPWP, file your SPT Tahunan by 31 March (individuals), and report all worldwide income. If you believe you qualify as a non-resident, document your position clearly — in writing — before any DJP inquiry arrives.
