## The Facts

Indonesia's tax residency threshold of 183 days within a 12-month period is enshrined in the Income Tax Law (UU PPh No. 36/2008) and remains in full effect in 2026. Any individual — regardless of nationality, visa type, or stated intention — who spends more than 183 days in Indonesia within a rolling 12-month window is classified as a domestic tax subject under Indonesian law.

The consequences are significant and frequently underestimated. Once classified as a tax resident, an individual is theoretically liable for Indonesian income tax on their worldwide income, not merely income sourced within Indonesia. Indonesia applies a progressive tax rate structure ranging from 5% on income up to IDR 60 million annually, scaling to 35% on income exceeding IDR 5 billion. For foreign nationals with substantial offshore income streams, this exposure can be material.

The 183-day count is cumulative and does not require consecutive days. Days spent in Indonesia across multiple visa entries — including tourist stays on Visa on Arrival, social-cultural visas, and business visas — all count toward the threshold. Border runs and short exits reset nothing in the eyes of the Directorate General of Taxes (DJP) if the total days within a 12-month window remain above the limit.

In practice, enforcement against individual foreign nationals has historically been uneven, but that landscape is shifting. Indonesia's participation in the OECD's Common Reporting Standard (CRS) since 2018 means foreign financial institutions are sharing account data with DJP. Cross-border income is increasingly visible to Indonesian tax authorities, narrowing the window for unintentional non-compliance to go undetected.

There is a partial exception worth noting: Indonesia has Double Tax Avoidance Agreements (P3B) with over 70 countries. Where a valid tax treaty exists between Indonesia and an expat's home country, tie-breaker rules may determine that the individual remains a tax resident of their home jurisdiction even after exceeding 183 days in Indonesia. However, invoking treaty protection is not automatic — it requires proactive filing, proper documentation, and in many cases, formal coordination between two tax authorities. The burden of proof rests with the taxpayer.
